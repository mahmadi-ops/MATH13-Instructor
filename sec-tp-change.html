    Subsection 4.6.3 Estimating the Change in a Particular Direction

    Before turning to functions of two variables, it is worth recalling the analogous idea from single-variable calculus. For a differentiable function \(y = f(x)\text{,}\) moving a small distance \(dx\) away from a point \(x\) changes the height of the curve by an amount that can be estimated from the slope \(f'(x)\text{:}\) the resulting differential is
    \begin{equation} df = f'(x)\,dx.\tag{4.6.8} \end{equation}
    As Figure 4.6.6 shows, this differential closely approximates the actual change in the function, \(\Delta f = f(x+dx) - f(x)\text{,}\) as long as \(dx\) is small.
    🔗
    The same question arises for a function of two variables. The difference is that near a point in the plane there is no longer a single direction to move in — moving away from a point requires both a distance and a direction. Consider the function \(z = f(x,y)\text{,}\) a point \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) in its domain, and a unit vector \(\hat{u} = u_1\,\hat{\imath} + u_2\,\hat{\jmath}\text{.}\) The question is:
    🔗
    How can we estimate the change in \(f\) (\(\Delta f\)) as a result of moving a small distance (\(ds\)) away from \(P_0\) in the direction \(\hat{u}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Moving away from \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) by the distance \(ds\) in the direction \(\hat{u}\) is the displacement \(\hat{u}\,ds = u_1\,ds\,\hat{\imath} + u_2\,ds\,\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) with \(\|\hat{u}\,ds\| = ds\text{,}\) and it takes us to the point \(P(x_0 + u_1\,ds,\; y_0 + u_2\,ds)\text{.}\) As Figure 4.6.7 shows, this causes the change
    \begin{equation} \Delta f = f(x_0 + u_1\,ds,\; y_0 + u_2\,ds) - f(x_0, y_0)\tag{4.6.9} \end{equation}
    in the value of the function.
    🔗
    We know from (4.5.5) that the rate of change of \(f\) at a point \(P_0\) in the direction of \(\hat{u}\) is given by the directional derivative
    \begin{equation} \left(\frac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat{u}, P_0} = \nabla f \Big|_{P_0} \cdot \hat{u}.\tag{4.6.10} \end{equation}
    Hence, we can estimate the change in \(f\) as
    \begin{equation} \Delta f \approx df = \left(\nabla f \Big|_{P_0} \cdot \hat{u}\right) ds.\tag{4.6.11} \end{equation}
    The quantity \(df\) is called the differential of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 4.6.8. Estimating the Change in a Given Direction.

    Consider the function \(f(x,y) = 1 + x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) the point \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\) and the unit vector \(\hat{u} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,\hat{\imath} + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,\hat{\jmath}\text{.}\) Estimate the change in the value of \(f\) as a result of moving away from \(P_0(1,1)\) in the direction of \(\hat{u}\) by \(0.1\) units.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that from the question we know \(ds = 0.1\) units. Our goal is to estimate the change in \(f\text{.}\) We compute the gradient of \(f\) and evaluate it at \(P_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= 2x\,\hat{\imath} + 2y\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \nabla f \Big|_{(1,1)} \amp= 2\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
    By (4.6.11),
    \begin{align} df \amp= \left(\nabla f \Big|_{P_0} \cdot \hat{u}\right) ds\notag\\ \amp= \left(\langle 2, 2 \rangle \cdot \Bigl\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} \Bigr\rangle\right)(0.1)\notag\\ \amp= 0.2\sqrt 2 \approx 0.28 \text{ units}.\tag{4.6.12} \end{align}
    This means the change in \(f\) is approximately \(0.28\) units. See Figure 4.6.9.
    🔗
    For comparison, the exact change is
    \begin{align*} \Delta f \amp= f\left(1 + \frac{0.1}{\sqrt 2},\, 1 + \frac{0.1}{\sqrt 2}\right) - f(1,1)\\ \amp= \left(3 + 0.2\sqrt 2 + 0.01\right) - 3 = 0.2\sqrt 2 + 0.01, \end{align*}
    so the estimate \(df\) is off by only \(0.01\) units. Figure 4.6.10 zooms in on the stretch just past \(P_0\) and shows this gap between the tangent line and the surface.
