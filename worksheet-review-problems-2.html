    1. Describing More Equations and Inequalities Geometrically.

    Describe the geometrical meaning of the following equalities and inequalities.
    1. \(\displaystyle y = -2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle y = -2,\; z = 1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + z^2 = 9\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + z^2 \le 9,\; y \ge -1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle y^2 + z^2 = 4\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(\displaystyle y^2 + z^2 = 4,\; 0 \le x \le 5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \(\displaystyle x = y\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. This is the set of all points whose \(y\)-coordinate equals \(-2\text{,}\) that is \(\{(x,y,z) \in \R^3 : y = -2\}\text{.}\) Both \(x\) and \(z\) are unrestricted, so it is a plane parallel to the \(xz\)-plane, sitting two units to the negative side of it, shown in Figure 5.9.22.
    🔗
    B. This is the intersection of the two planes \(y = -2\) and \(z = 1\text{,}\) which is a line. Only \(x\) is unrestricted, so the line is parallel to the \(x\)-axis and passes through the point \((0,-2,1)\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 5.9.23.
    🔗
    C. Comparing with the standard equation of a sphere, this is the sphere of radius \(3\) centered at \((2,-1,0)\text{.}\) Note the center lies in the \(xy\)-plane, and since the radius is \(3\) the sphere reaches from \(z = -3\) up to \(z = 3\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 5.9.24.
    🔗
    D. Replacing the equality by \(\le\) gives the solid ball: every point whose distance from \((2,-1,0)\) is at most \(3\text{.}\) The extra condition \(y \ge -1\) keeps only the points on one side of the plane \(y = -1\text{.}\) That plane passes through the center of the ball, so exactly half of the ball survives: the region is a solid half-ball of radius \(3\text{,}\) whose flat face is the disk of radius \(3\) cut out of the plane \(y = -1\text{,}\) shown in Figure 5.9.25.
    🔗
    E. In the \(yz\)-plane, \(y^2 + z^2 = 4\) is a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin. In space \(x\) is unrestricted, so the equation represents an infinitely long cylinder of radius \(2\) whose axis is the \(x\)-axis. Had the missing variable been \(z\) instead, the axis would have been the \(z\)-axis: the axis of the cylinder is always the axis of the variable that does not appear. The left half of Figure 5.9.26 shows this cylinder.
    🔗
    F. The extra condition \(0 \le x \le 5\) cuts the cylinder of part E down to a piece of length \(5\text{,}\) running from the circle in the plane \(x = 0\) to the circle in the plane \(x = 5\text{,}\) as on the right of Figure 5.9.26. It is a tube, not a solid: the two end disks are not included, since the equation forces \(y^2 + z^2 = 4\) exactly.
    🔗
    G. The condition \(x = y\) says nothing about \(z\text{,}\) so whenever a point satisfies it, so does the whole vertical line through that point. In the \(xy\)-plane the equation \(x = y\) describes a line through the origin, and sweeping that line vertically gives a plane: the plane containing the \(z\)-axis that cuts the \(xy\)-plane along the line \(y = x\text{,}\) shown in Figure 5.9.27. It is not parallel to any coordinate plane, unlike part A.

    2. A Solid Bounded by a Cylinder and a Plane.

    Consider the solid region bounded by the three surfaces
    \begin{equation*} z = 0, \qquad x^2+y^2 = 4, \qquad x+y+z = 4, \end{equation*}
    restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{.}\) Describe each of the three bounding surfaces, and describe the solid region they enclose.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equation \(x^2+y^2=4\) is missing \(z\text{,}\) so it is the infinite cylinder of radius \(2\) whose axis is the \(z\)-axis. The equation \(z=0\) is the \(xy\)-plane, and \(x+y+z=4\) is a plane tilted with respect to all three coordinate axes.
    🔗
    Solving the third equation for \(z\) gives \(z = 4-x-y\text{.}\) Over the disk \(x^2+y^2 \le 4\) enclosed by the cylinder, this height stays positive: the largest value of \(x+y\) on that disk is \(2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83\text{,}\) attained in the direction \((1,1)\text{,}\) so \(z = 4-x-y \ge 4-2\sqrt2 > 0\) throughout the disk. The tilted plane therefore never dips below the \(xy\)-plane over the region enclosed by the cylinder.
    🔗
    The solid is the piece of the solid cylinder \(x^2+y^2 \le 4\) lying between the flat base \(z=0\) and the slanted lid \(z=4-x-y\text{:}\) an oblique, truncated cylinder whose base is the disk of radius \(2\) in the \(xy\)-plane and whose slanted top is an ellipse, shown in Figure 5.9.28.

    3.

    The vectors \(2\,\mathbf i + 3\,\mathbf j\text{,}\) \(4\,\mathbf i + \mathbf j\) and \(5\,\mathbf i + y\,\mathbf j\) have their tails at the origin. Find the value of \(y\) that will make their heads collinear.
    🔗
    Three points are collinear when they all lie on one straight line. Two points always do, so the condition is a condition on the third: it has to lie on the line already determined by the other two.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The heads are the points \((2,3)\text{,}\) \((4,1)\text{,}\) and \((5,y)\text{,}\) and we want all three on a single line. Two of them are already given, so the line is fixed: the line through the first two has slope
    \begin{equation*} m = \frac{1-3}{4-2} = -1. \end{equation*}
    For \((5,y)\) to lie on that same line, the slope from \((4,1)\) to \((5,y)\) must also be \(-1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y-1}{5-4} = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; y - 1 = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Equivalently, in vector language: the three heads are collinear exactly when the displacement vectors between them are parallel. Here \(\langle 4,1\rangle - \langle 2,3\rangle = \langle 2,-2\rangle\) and \(\langle 5,y\rangle - \langle 4,1\rangle = \langle 1,y-1\rangle\text{,}\) and these are parallel exactly when \(\langle 1, y-1\rangle = \tfrac12\langle 2,-2\rangle\text{,}\) that is \(y - 1 = -1\text{,}\) giving \(y = 0\) again.

    4.

    Two vectors \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are drawn with a common tail; \(\vec u\) points up and to the right, \(\vec v\) points up and to the left, and the angle between them is slightly larger than a right angle. Sketch and clearly label a diagram showing \(\vec u\text{,}\) \(\vec v\text{,}\) and \(\vec v - \vec u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To make the picture concrete let us take \(\vec u = \left\langle 4, 2 \right\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \left\langle -3, 5 \right\rangle\text{,}\) which have roughly the directions described. Everything below is a statement about the geometry, so the same picture is correct for any pair of vectors in that configuration.
    🔗
    The difference \(\vec v - \vec u\) is the vector that, added to \(\vec u\text{,}\) gives \(\vec v\text{.}\) So when \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are drawn from a common tail, \(\vec v - \vec u\) is the arrow that runs from the tip of \(\vec u\) to the tip of \(\vec v\text{.}\) (Here it is \(\left\langle -7, 3 \right\rangle\text{.}\)) It is easy to get this backwards; the check is that \(\vec u + (\vec v - \vec u) = \vec v\text{.}\) See Figure 5.9.29.

    5.

    Find the projection of \(-\mathbf i + 5\,\mathbf j - 2\,\mathbf k\) on \(3\,\mathbf i - \mathbf j\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(\vec u = \langle -1, 5, -2\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \langle 3, -1, 0\rangle\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec u \cdot \vec v = -3 - 5 + 0 = -8, \qquad \|\vec v\|^2 = 9 + 1 + 0 = 10, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}\vec u \amp = \left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{\|\vec v\|^2}\right)\vec v = -\frac{8}{10}\langle 3, -1, 0\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac{12}{5}, \frac45, 0\right\rangle = -\frac{12}{5}\,\mathbf i + \frac45\,\mathbf j. \end{align*}
    The negative scalar tells us the projection points opposite to \(\vec v\text{,}\) i.e. the angle between \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) is obtuse.

    6.

    Let \(\vec v\) be the vector from \((-4,3,-1)\) to \((-2,-1,3)\text{.}\)
    1. Find a non-zero unit vector that is orthogonal to \(\vec v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the angle between \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w = 3\mathbf i - \mathbf k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First,
    \begin{align*} \vec v \amp = \left\langle -2-(-4),\ -1-3,\ 3-(-1) \right\rangle = \left\langle 2,\ -4,\ 4 \right\rangle ,\\ \|\vec v\| \amp = \sqrt{4+16+16} = \sqrt{36} = 6 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    (a) We need any non-zero \(\vec u\) with \(\vec u \cdot \vec v = 0\text{.}\) Writing \(\vec u = \left\langle a,b,c\right\rangle\text{,}\) the condition is
    \begin{equation*} 2a - 4b + 4c = 0 . \end{equation*}
    This is one equation in three unknowns, so there are infinitely many answers; we just pick a convenient one. Taking \(c = 0\) gives \(2a = 4b\text{,}\) so \(a = 2\text{,}\) \(b = 1\) works:
    \begin{equation*} \vec u = \left\langle 2,\ 1,\ 0 \right\rangle , \qquad \vec u \cdot \vec v = 4 - 4 + 0 = 0 . \end{equation*}
    Finally we normalize:
    \begin{equation*} \hat u = \frac{\vec u}{\|\vec u\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left\langle 2,\ 1,\ 0 \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt5},\ \frac{1}{\sqrt5},\ 0 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (b) With \(\vec w = \left\langle 3, 0, -1 \right\rangle\) we have
    \begin{align*} \vec v \cdot \vec w \amp = (2)(3) + (-4)(0) + (4)(-1) = 6 - 4 = 2 ,\\ \|\vec w\| \amp = \sqrt{9+0+1} = \sqrt{10} . \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \cos\theta = \frac{\vec v \cdot \vec w}{\|\vec v\|\,\|\vec w\|} = \frac{2}{6\sqrt{10}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.1054 , \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{3\sqrt{10}}\right) \approx 1.465 \text{ radians} \approx 83.9^{\circ} . \end{equation*}
    The angle is slightly less than a right angle, which matches the fact that the dot product is positive but small.

    7.

    Let \(A = (0,0,1)\) and \(B = (0,2,3)\text{.}\) Find an equation for the set of all points \(P = (x,y,z)\) such that \(\vec{AP}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec{BP}\text{.}\) Then sketch the resulting surface in three dimensions.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The two vectors are
    \begin{align*} \vec{AP} \amp = \left\langle x-0,\ y-0,\ z-1 \right\rangle = \left\langle x,\ y,\ z-1 \right\rangle ,\\ \vec{BP} \amp = \left\langle x,\ y-2,\ z-3 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Orthogonality means the dot product vanishes:
    \begin{align*} \vec{AP}\cdot\vec{BP} \amp = 0\\ x^2 + y(y-2) + (z-1)(z-3) \amp = 0\\ x^2 + y^2 - 2y + z^2 - 4z + 3 \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now complete the square in \(y\) and in \(z\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} x^2 + \left(y^2 - 2y + 1\right) + \left(z^2 - 4z + 4\right) \amp = -3 + 1 + 4\\ x^2 + (y-1)^2 + (z-2)^2 \amp = 2 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the sphere of radius \(\sqrt2\) centred at \((0,1,2)\text{.}\) That centre is exactly the midpoint of \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) and the radius is exactly half of \(\|\vec{AB}\| = \|\langle 0,2,2\rangle\| = 2\sqrt2\text{.}\) This is the three-dimensional version of the familiar fact from plane geometry: the points from which a given segment subtends a right angle form the circle having that segment as a diameter. See Figure 5.9.30.

    8.

    Two vectors \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are drawn with a common tail; \(\vec u\) points up and to the right, \(\vec v\) points up and to the left, and the angle between them is slightly larger than a right angle. Sketch and clearly label a diagram showing \(\vec u\text{,}\) \(\vec v\text{,}\) and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec u}\vec v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To make the picture concrete let us take \(\vec u = \left\langle 4, 2 \right\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \left\langle -3, 5 \right\rangle\text{,}\) which have roughly the directions described. Everything below is a statement about the geometry, so the same picture is correct for any pair of vectors in that configuration.

    Because the angle between \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) is obtuse, the dot product is negative and the projection points in the direction opposite to \(\vec u\text{.}\) This is the feature of the picture the problem is testing. See Figure 5.9.31.

    9.

    Suppose \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are non-zero vectors satisfying
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\| = 5, \qquad \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\| = 13, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2 = 97, \qquad \|\mathbf u\| = 7 . \end{equation*}
    Without finding the coordinates of either vector:
    1. Compute \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find \(\|\mathbf v\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Determine whether the angle is acute, right, or obtuse.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The whole problem rests on one idea: the square of a length is a dot product, \(\|\mathbf w\|^2 = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w\text{.}\) Expanding with the distributive property,
    \begin{align*} \|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\|^2 \amp = (\mathbf u + \mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = \|\mathbf u\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v + \|\mathbf v\|^2 ,\\ \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|^2 \amp = (\mathbf u - \mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u - \mathbf v) = \|\mathbf u\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v + \|\mathbf v\|^2 . \end{align*}
    These two identities are the algebraic form of the parallelogram in Figure 5.9.32: the diagonals of the parallelogram built on \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are \(\mathbf u + \mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u - \mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Subtracting the second identity from the first makes the \(\|\mathbf u\|^2\) and \(\|\mathbf v\|^2\) terms cancel, leaving only the dot product:
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\|^2 - \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|^2 = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v . \end{equation*}
    Substituting the two given lengths,
    \begin{align*} 5^2 - 13^2 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v ,\\ 25 - 169 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v ,\\ -144 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v , \end{align*}
    and therefore \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = -36\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It is worth pausing to check that the data are consistent. Adding the two identities instead of subtracting gives the parallelogram law,
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\|^2 + \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|^2 = 2\left(\|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2\right) , \end{equation*}
    and indeed \(5^2 + 13^2 = 25 + 169 = 194 = 2(97)\text{,}\) exactly as it should be.
    🔗
    (b) We are told \(\|\mathbf u\| = 7\) and \(\|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2 = 97\text{,}\) so

    12. *Circular Motion from a Perpendicular Force.

    Starred problem. This one is for the interested reader. It will not be examined.
    🔗
    Through this guided exercise, we prove that a force of constant magnitude that always points perpendicular to the velocity drives the particle around a circle. We then apply it to a charged particle in a magnetic field.
    🔗
    Both of the products you have met so far appear, and they do genuinely different jobs. Watch for them:
    • the dot product is used, through \(\vec F\cdot\vec v\text{,}\) to determine whether the speed of a particle acted upon by a perpendicular force can change,
      🔗
      🔗
    • the cross product is used to determine the direction and magnitude of the force that keeps a charged particle moving in a uniform magnetic field.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    A particle of mass \(m\) moves under a force \(\vec F\) of constant magnitude that is always perpendicular to its velocity \(\vec v\text{.}\) Show that the particle’s speed is constant.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\vec F\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \vec F\cdot\vec v = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now consider the square of the speed, \(v^2 = \vec v\cdot\vec v\text{.}\) Using the product rule for the dot product
     2 
    The product rule holds for the dot product just as it does for ordinary multiplication: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\vec u\cdot\vec v\right) = \dfrac{d\vec u}{dt}\cdot\vec v + \vec u\cdot\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt}\text{.}\) Taking \(\vec u = \vec v\) makes the two terms equal, which gives the step used here, \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\vec v\cdot\vec v\right) = 2\,\vec v\cdot\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt}\text{.}\)
    and then Newton’s second law \(m\,\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} = \vec F\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt}\left(v^2\right) \amp = \frac{d}{dt} \left(\vec v\cdot\vec v\right) = 2\,\vec v\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{2}{m}\,\vec v\cdot\vec F = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    So \(v^2\) never changes, and hence neither does the speed \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Why the dot product is the right tool here. The quantity \(\vec F\cdot\vec v\) is the rate at which the force does work on the particle. A force with a forward component speeds the particle up; a force with a backward component slows it down. Only the part of \(\vec F\) lying along \(\vec v\) can change the speed, and the dot product is exactly the mathematical tool that isolates that part. When it vanishes, can only change the direction of the velocity but not its magnitude, and so can only turn the particle, while the speed remains constant. See Figure 5.9.34.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    During a short time interval \(\Delta t\text{,}\) show that the direction of the velocity turns through the angle
    \begin{equation*} \Delta\theta = \frac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    (Hint: draw the two velocity vectors, which have equal length, from a common tail.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the previous task the velocity never changes length; it only swings around. So \(\vec v(t)\) and \(\vec v(t+\Delta t)\) are two vectors of the same length \(v\text{,}\) separated by the small angle \(\Delta\theta\) we are after. Drawn from a common tail they form an isosceles triangle whose third side is \(\Delta\vec v = \vec v(t+\Delta t) - \vec v(t)\text{,}\) as in Figure 5.9.35.
    🔗
    The tips of the two vectors both lie on a circle of radius \(v\text{.}\) The straight side \(\Delta\vec v\) is the chord cutting off the arc of angle \(\Delta\theta\) on that circle, and for a small angle a chord is very nearly the arc it subtends:
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\Delta\vec v\right\| = 2v\sin\frac{\Delta\theta}{2} \approx v\,\Delta\theta\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    On the other hand Newton’s second law over the short interval gives
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\Delta\vec v\right\| \approx \frac{F}{m}\,\Delta t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Equating the two expressions for \(\left\|\Delta\vec v\right\|\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} v\,\Delta\theta = \frac{F}{m}\,\Delta t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which is what we wanted. Notice the rate of turning \(\Delta\theta/\Delta t = F/(mv)\) is a constant, since \(F\text{,}\) \(m\) and \(v\) all are: the particle swings around at a steady angular rate.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Combine \(\Delta s = v\,\Delta t\) with the arc-length formula \(\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta\) to prove that
    \begin{equation*} R = \frac{mv^2}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    In time \(\Delta t\) the particle covers the arc length \(\Delta s = v\,\Delta t\text{.}\) If that arc bends through the angle \(\Delta\theta\text{,}\) then the radius \(R\) of the arc is defined by \(\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta\text{.}\) Solving for \(R\) and substituting both expressions,
    \begin{equation*} R = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta\theta} = \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\dfrac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t} = \frac{mv^2}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Conclude that the trajectory is a circle.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The quantity \(R = mv^2/F\) is built from \(m\text{,}\) \(v\) and \(F\text{,}\) every one of which is constant — \(v\) because of the first task, and \(F\) by hypothesis. So the path bends through equal angles over equal arc lengths, everywhere along the trajectory: its radius of curvature is the same constant \(R\) at every point.
    🔗
    A plane curve with constant radius of curvature is a circle. Concretely, the centre of the turn always lies a fixed distance \(R\) from the particle in the direction of \(\vec F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    A particle of charge \(q\) enters a uniform magnetic field \(\vec B\) with its velocity perpendicular to the field. The magnetic force is
    \begin{equation*} \vec F_B = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Show that this force has magnitude \(|q|\,vB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The length of a cross product is \(\left\|\vec v\times\vec B\right\| = vB\sin\alpha\text{,}\) where \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(\vec v\) and \(\vec B\text{.}\) Here the velocity is perpendicular to the field, so \(\alpha = 90^\circ\) and \(\sin\alpha = 1\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\vec F_B\right\| = |q|\left\|\vec v\times\vec B\right\| = |q|\,vB\sin 90^\circ = |q|\,vB\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(v\) is constant (we are about to confirm this) and \(B\) is uniform, this magnitude is constant — the first hypothesis of part a.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Explain why this force is always perpendicular to the velocity — not just at the initial instant, but at every moment of the motion.
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is the defining property of the cross product: \(\vec v\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to both factors, whatever they happen to be. So \(\vec F_B = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\) automatically, at every instant, with no computation and no special assumption about where the particle is or which way it is heading.
    🔗
    If you would like it verified rather than quoted, the dot product does it in one line.
     3 
    Two facts are doing the work. First, the scalar triple product \(\left(\vec a\times\vec b\right)\cdot\vec c\) equals the determinant whose rows are \(\vec a\text{,}\) \(\vec b\) and \(\vec c\text{.}\) Second, a determinant with two equal rows is zero, since interchanging those two rows both leaves the determinant unchanged and reverses its sign.
    Taking \(\vec c = \vec v\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec F_B\cdot\vec v = q\left(\vec v\times\vec B\right)\cdot\vec v = q\begin{vmatrix} v_1 \amp v_2 \amp v_3 \\ B_1 \amp B_2 \amp B_3 \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp v_3 \end{vmatrix} = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So such a magnetic field can never change the speed of a charged particle, no matter how strong it is. Here the magnetic force does no work and it solely changes the direction of the velocity.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    Deduce that the particle moves in a circle, and find the radius of that circle.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The previous two tasks establish exactly the two hypotheses of part a: the magnetic force has constant magnitude \(|q|vB\text{,}\) and it is always perpendicular to the velocity. The argument of parts a–d therefore applies verbatim, and the particle travels in a circle at constant speed.
    🔗
    For the radius, put \(F = |q|vB\) into \(R = mv^2/F\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{mv^2}{|q|vB} = \frac{mv}{|q|B}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, the magnetic force supplies precisely the inward force \(mv^2/r\) that circular motion demands:
    \begin{equation*} |q|vB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r = \frac{mv}{|q|B}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    See Figure 5.9.37. Faster particles and heavier particles travel on wider circles; stronger fields and larger charges wind them tighter.

    🔗
    A particle is projected from \(O\) with speed \(v_0\) at an elevation \(\theta\) to the horizontal, as in Figure 5.9.38. At time \(t\) it is at the point \(P\text{,}\) moving with speed \(v\) in a direction making an angle \(\phi\) with the horizontal. Show that
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{\,v_0^2 - 2\,v_0\,g\,t\sin\theta + g^2 t^2\,} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \tan\phi = \frac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Take the origin at \(O\) with horizontal and vertical axes. The coordinates of \(P\) at time \(t\) are
    \begin{equation*} x = v_0\cos\theta\;t\text{,} \qquad y = v_0\sin\theta\;t - \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The horizontal and vertical components of the velocity are the time derivatives of these, so
    \begin{equation*} v\cos\phi = \frac{dx}{dt} = v_0\cos\theta\text{,} \qquad v\sin\phi = \frac{dy}{dt} = v_0\sin\theta - g\,t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Squaring and adding eliminates \(\phi\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} v^2 = v_0^2\cos^2\theta + \bigl(v_0\sin\theta - g\,t\bigr)^2 = v_0^2 - 2\,v_0\,g\,t\sin\theta + g^2 t^2\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which gives the speed at time \(t\text{.}\) Dividing the vertical component by the horizontal component gives the direction,
    \begin{equation*} \tan\phi = \frac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    that is, \(\phi = \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\right)\text{.}\)

    15. *A collision on the directrix.

    Starred problem. This one is for the interested reader. It will not be examined.
    🔗
    Two objects move under gravity. The first is the projectile of the two previous exercises: launched from \(O\) with speed \(v_0\) at an elevation \(\theta\) to the horizontal. The second is a ball released from rest at a point \(B\) on the directrix, the horizontal line at height \(\dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}\) above \(O\text{.}\) We want the two to collide at the moment the projectile is at half of its maximum height, on the way down. See Figure 5.9.39.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that the projectile is at half of its maximum height at the two times
    \begin{equation*} t_\pm = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\left(1 \pm \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \end{equation*}
    and say which of these is on the descending branch.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertical motion is \(y = v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\text{.}\) It is greatest when \(\dot y = v_0\sin\theta - g t = 0\text{,}\) that is at \(t = v_0\sin\theta/g\text{,}\) which gives the maximum height
    \begin{equation*} H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Setting \(y = H/2\) gives the quadratic equation
    \begin{equation*} t^2 - \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\,t + \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g^2} = 0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    whose discriminant is \(2v_0^2\sin^2\theta/g^2\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} t_\pm = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g} \pm \frac{\sqrt2\,v_0\sin\theta}{2g} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\left(1 \pm \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    These straddle the apex time \(v_0\sin\theta/g\) symmetrically, as they must. The descending crossing is the later one,
    \begin{equation*} T = t_+ = \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0\sin\theta}{2g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the point \(B\) on the directrix from which the ball must be released.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Released from rest, the ball has no horizontal velocity, so it falls along a vertical line: \(B\) must be directly above the collision point \(C\text{.}\) The projectile’s horizontal coordinate at time \(T\) is
    \begin{equation*} x_C = v_0\cos\theta\cdot T = \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{4g}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    so the release point is
    \begin{equation*} B = \left(\frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{4g},\; \frac{v_0^2}{2g}\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    In terms of the range \(R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\) this is \(x_C = \frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}R \approx 0.854\,R\)—comfortably past the apex at \(R/2\) and short of the landing point, as Figure 5.9.39 shows.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find how long after the projectile is launched the ball must be released.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The depth of \(C\) below the directrix is
    \begin{equation*} h = \frac{v_0^2}{2g} - \frac{H}{2} = \frac{v_0^2\left(2-\sin^2\theta\right)}{4g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Falling from rest through \(h\) takes a time \(\tau\) with \(h = \tfrac12 g\tau^2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \tau = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \frac{v_0}{g}\sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The ball must therefore be released
    \begin{equation*} t_{\text{rel}} = T - \tau = \frac{v_0}{g}\left[\frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)\sin\theta}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\right] \end{equation*}
    seconds after the projectile is launched.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Show that at the instant of the collision the two objects have exactly the same speed.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Worksheet Exercise 5.9.14, a projectile at depth \(h\) below its directrix has speed \(v_{\text{proj}} = \sqrt{2gh}\text{.}\) The ball starts from rest on the directrix and has fallen the same depth \(h\text{,}\) so its speed is
    \begin{equation*} v_{\text{ball}} = g\tau = g\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{2gh}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The two are equal. This is not a coincidence of our particular choice of \(C\text{:}\) the projectile always carries exactly the speed it would have gained by falling freely from the directrix, and the ball is doing precisely that fall. Wherever on the path we arrange the collision, the two objects meet with the same speed, here
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{2gh} = v_0\sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Their directions of course differ: the ball moves straight down, while the projectile moves down and forward at the angle \(\phi\) of Worksheet Exercise 5.9.13.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Is \(t_{\text{rel}}\) always positive? Find the elevations \(\theta\) for which the ball is released after the projectile is launched, and explain what a negative \(t_{\text{rel}}\) would mean.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Requiring \(t_{\text{rel}} \geq 0\) means
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)\sin\theta}{2} \geq \sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and squaring gives \(\sin^2\theta\left(4+2\sqrt2\right) \geq 2\text{,}\) that is
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2\theta \geq \frac{1}{2+\sqrt2} = \frac{2-\sqrt2}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \theta \geq \arcsin\sqrt{\frac{2-\sqrt2}{2}} \approx 32.8^\circ\text{.} \end{equation*}
    For flatter launches the required release time is negative: the ball would have to be dropped before the projectile is fired, because a shallow trajectory reaches its (low) half-height point sooner than the ball can fall the long way down from the directrix.

    16.

    Consider the curve of intersection of the surfaces \(4x^2 + z^2 = 16\) and \(y = z\text{.}\)
    1. Parametrize the curve.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the projection of the curve onto the \(xy\)-plane, and give a Cartesian equation for it.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The first surface does not involve \(y\text{;}\) dividing by \(16\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{16} = 1, \end{equation*}
    which is an ellipse in the \(xz\)-plane with semi-axes \(2\) (in \(x\)) and \(4\) (in \(z\)). It is parametrized by \(x = 2\cos(t)\text{,}\) \(z = 4\sin(t)\text{.}\) The second surface simply forces \(y = z\text{,}\) so

    🔗
    (b) Projecting onto the \(xy\)-plane just means forgetting the \(z\)-coordinate, so the projected curve is parametrized by the first two components alone,
    \begin{equation*} x = 2\cos(t), \qquad y = 4\sin(t), \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \end{equation*}

    22. *A Parabolic Antenna.

    Starred problem. This one is for the interested reader. It will not be examined.
    🔗
    A parabolic antenna has the shape of a paraboloid of revolution: it is created by rotating part of a parabola around its axis. By the reflection property, all signals arriving parallel to the axis are concentrated at the focus after reflecting off the dish, so that is where the receiver is mounted. The axial cross-section of the dish is described by two measurements: the diameter \(d\) of the dish and its depth \(h\text{.}\) Placing the vertex at the origin with the dish opening upward, the cross-section is the parabola \(x^2 = 4py\text{,}\) and the rim passes through the points \(\left(\pm\tfrac{d}{2},\, h\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Consider a dish antenna with diameter \(d = 120\) cm and depth \(h = 20.3\) cm, suitable for the amateur radio band at \(5.76\) GHz.
    🔗

    (a)

    Determine the optimal location for the receiver, that is, find the distance from the vertex of the dish to the focus.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The receiver must sit at the focus \((0,p)\text{.}\) The rim point \(\left(\tfrac{d}{2}, h\right) = (60,\, 20.3)\) lies on the parabola \(x^2 = 4py\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} 60^2 = 4p(20.3) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \frac{3600}{81.2} \approx 44.3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The receiver should be mounted on the axis about \(44.3\) cm above the vertex of the dish. Note that \(p \gt h\) here, so the focus sits above the rim of this shallow dish.

    (b)

    Find the quadratic function \(y = f(x)\) (explicit form) that describes the curvature of the dish, and graph it (for example, in GeoGebra or Desmos).

    \begin{equation*} y = \frac{1}{4p}\,x^2 = \frac{20.3}{3600}\,x^2 \approx \frac{x^2}{177.3}, \qquad -60 \le x \le 60. \end{equation*}

    24. Which of These Are Hyperboloids?

    None of the equations below is in standard position. Complete the square where necessary, then select every equation whose surface is a hyperboloid, of one sheet or of two.
    🔗
    • \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - 2x = 0\)
    • Correct. Completing the square gives \((x-1)^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1\text{:}\) one negative square, equal to a positive constant, so a hyperboloid of one sheet.
    • \(x^2 + y^2 + 4z^2 - 4y = 0\)
    • No. This is \(x^2 + (y-2)^2 + 4z^2 = 4\) — every square is positive, so it is an ellipsoid.
    • \(x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = 1\)
    • Correct. Two negative squares against a positive constant gives a hyperboloid of two sheets.
    • \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0\)
    • No — and this is the trap. It has the same signs as the first option, but it equals \(0\) rather than a positive constant. That degenerates the hyperboloid into an elliptic cone, the surface the two families of hyperboloids approach asymptotically.
    • \(z + 1 = x^2 - y^2\)
    • No. The word “hyperbolic” is a red herring: this is linear in \(z\text{,}\) so it is a hyperbolic paraboloid (a saddle) with vertex at \((0,0,-1)\text{,}\) not a hyperboloid.
    Solution.
    Completing the square in the first and second,
    \begin{align*} x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - 2x \amp = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-1)^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1,\\ x^2 + y^2 + 4z^2 - 4y \amp = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + (y-2)^2 + 4z^2 = 4 . \end{align*}
    Reading off the signs: one negative square against \(+1\) is a hyperboloid of one sheet; two negative squares against \(+1\) is a hyperboloid of two sheets. So the first and third are hyperboloids. The second is an ellipsoid, the fourth equals \(0\) and so is a cone, and the fifth is linear in \(z\) and so is a hyperbolic paraboloid.

    27. Which Identity Fails?

    Exactly one of the following is not valid for all vectors \(\vec a, \vec b, \vec c\) in \(\R^3\text{.}\) Which one?
    🔗
    • \(\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec c\)
    • This one is valid. Both sides equal the determinant with rows \(\vec a,\vec b,\vec c\text{,}\) so the dot and the cross may be interchanged.
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c\)
    • Correct — the cross product is not associative. Take \(\vec a = \vec b = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec c = \mathbf j\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i\times(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j) = \mathbf i\times\mathbf k = -\mathbf j, \qquad (\mathbf i\times\mathbf i)\times\mathbf j = \vec 0\times\mathbf j = \vec 0 . \end{equation*}
      The left side lies in the plane of \(\vec b\) and \(\vec c\text{;}\) the right side lies in the plane of \(\vec a\) and \(\vec b\text{.}\) There is no reason for those to agree.
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b + \vec c) = \vec a\times\vec b + \vec a\times\vec c\)
    • This one is valid — the cross product is distributive over addition. (It is only associativity that fails.)
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\cdot\vec c)\,\vec b - (\vec a\cdot\vec b)\,\vec c\)
    • This one is valid: it is the “BAC-CAB” triple product expansion. It looks the most exotic of the four, which is exactly why it is worth remembering that it is true.
