Example 4.4.10. Pulling an Americano.

Some espresso machines make an Americano by adding hot water to the cup while the shot is still pouring. The cup now holds the espresso of Example 4.4.6 together with the added water, so its concentration is
\begin{equation*} C(m,V,W) = \frac{m}{V + W}, \end{equation*}
where \(m\) is the mass of dissolved coffee solids in the cup (in grams), \(V\) is the volume of espresso (in milliliters), and \(W\) is the volume of added hot water (in milliliters). All three change during the shot, so \(m = m(t)\text{,}\) \(V = V(t)\) and \(W = W(t)\text{,}\) with \(t\) in seconds. At \(t = 20\) s,
\begin{align*} m = 4\,\text{g}, \amp \quad \frac{dm}{dt} = 0.12\,\text{g/s},\\ V = 40\,\text{mL}, \amp \quad \frac{dV}{dt} = 1.6\,\text{mL/s},\\ W = 40\,\text{mL}, \amp \quad \frac{dW}{dt} = 2\,\text{mL/s}. \end{align*}
At \(t = 20\) s, is the cup getting stronger or weaker, and how fast?
🔗
Solution.
Step 1: Dependence. \(C\) depends on \(m\text{,}\) \(V\) and \(W\text{,}\) and each of those depends on \(t\text{,}\) as the tree diagram below shows.

Step 2: Chain rule. There are three paths from \(C\) down to \(t\text{,}\) so (4.4.2), with \(C\) in place of \(w\text{,}\) has three terms:
\begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{\partial C}{\partial m}\frac{dm}{dt} + \frac{\partial C}{\partial V}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp \quad + \frac{\partial C}{\partial W}\frac{dW}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{V+W}\frac{dm}{dt}\\ \amp \quad - \frac{m}{(V+W)^2}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp \quad - \frac{m}{(V+W)^2}\frac{dW}{dt}. \end{align*}
🔗
Step 3: Plug in. First, \(C = \frac{4}{40 + 40} = 0.05\,\text{g/mL}\text{,}\) about \(5\%\text{,}\) half the strength of the straight shot. Then
\begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{0.12}{80} - \frac{4}{80^2}\,(1.6)\\ \amp \quad - \frac{4}{80^2}\,(2)\\ \amp = 0.0015 - 0.001 - 0.00125\\ \amp = -0.00075\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}. \end{align*}
🔗
Step 4: Interpret. The concentration is decreasing by about \(0.00075\) g/mL (\(0.075\) percentage point) per second. The solids path adds \(+0.0015\text{,}\) and the two dilution paths take away \(0.001\) (espresso) and \(0.00125\) (water), so dilution wins.
🔗
Why? As in Example 4.4.6, write \(m' = \frac{dm}{dt}\text{,}\) \(V' = \frac{dV}{dt}\text{,}\) \(W' = \frac{dW}{dt}\text{,}\) and factor the derivative:
\begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{m' - C\,(V' + W')}{V+W}\\ \amp = \frac{V'+W'}{V+W}\left(\frac{m'}{V'+W'} - C\right). \end{align*}
What enters the cup now is espresso and water together, and \(\frac{m'}{V'+W'}\) is the concentration of that combined stream. Here it is \(0.12/3.6 \approx 0.033\,\text{g/mL}\) (\(3.3\%\)), weaker than the \(5\%\) already in the cup, so the cup gets weaker. The espresso stream by itself, at \(7.5\%\text{,}\) is stronger than the cup: it is the added water that tips the balance.
