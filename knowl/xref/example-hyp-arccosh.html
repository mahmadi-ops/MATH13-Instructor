Example 1.4.2. Computing \(\cosh^{-1}(x)\).

Show that the inverse hyperbolic cosine can be written in terms of the natural logarithm as
\begin{equation*} \cosh^{-1}(x) = \ln\left(x + \sqrt{x^2-1}\right), \qquad x \geq 1. \end{equation*}
🔗
Solution.
Since \(\cosh\) is even, it becomes one-to-one only after we restrict it to \(x \geq 0\text{,}\) where it increases from \(1\) to \(\infty\text{.}\) Set \(y = \cosh(x)\) and solve for \(x\text{.}\) Multiplying the numerator and the denominator by \(e^{x}\) gives
\begin{equation*} y = \cosh(x) = \frac{e^{x} + e^{-x}}{2}\cdot\frac{e^{x}}{e^{x}} = \frac{e^{2x}+1}{2e^{x}}. \end{equation*}
🔗
Clearing the denominator gives \(2y e^{x} = e^{2x} + 1\text{,}\) and the substitution \(u = e^{x}\) turns this into a quadratic equation in \(u\text{:}\)
\begin{align*} 2yu \amp = u^2 + 1\\ u^2 - 2yu + 1 \amp = 0. \end{align*}
🔗
By the quadratic formula,
\begin{equation*} u = \frac{2y \pm \sqrt{4y^2-4}}{2} = y \pm \sqrt{y^2-1}, \end{equation*}
that is, \(e^{x} = y \pm \sqrt{y^2-1}\text{.}\) Interchanging the names of the two variables, so that \(y = \cosh^{-1}(x)\) is the inverse function, we get
\begin{equation*} e^{y} = x \pm \sqrt{x^2-1}. \end{equation*}
🔗
It remains to decide which sign to take, and the convention is to choose \(y \geq 0\text{,}\) matching the restriction made above. Note that
\begin{equation*} x - \sqrt{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{x + \sqrt{x^2-1}} \leq 1, \qquad x \geq 1, \end{equation*}
so the minus sign would give \(y = \ln\left(x - \sqrt{x^2-1}\right) \leq 0\text{.}\) Since we are looking for \(y \geq 0\text{,}\) we choose the plus sign:
\begin{equation} y = \cosh^{-1}(x) = \ln\left(x + \sqrt{x^2-1}\right).\tag{1.4.1} \end{equation}
