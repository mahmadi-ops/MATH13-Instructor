Example 4.8.7. Absolute Extrema on a Circle.

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 5\text{,}\) given the constraint \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
Completing the squares shows that
\begin{equation} f(x,y) = (x-1)^2 + (y+1)^2 + 3,\tag{4.8.9} \end{equation}
so the graph of \(f\) is a paraboloid with vertex at \((1,-1,3)\text{.}\) The constraint can be thought of as \(g(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4 = 0\text{,}\) which is a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin in the \(xy\)-plane. Restricting \(f\) to this circle traces a curve on the paraboloid, and the goal is to find the highest and lowest points of that curve. By (4.8.9), the level curves of \(f\) are the circles \((x-1)^2 + (y+1)^2 = c - 3\) centered at \((1,-1)\text{,}\) and we are looking for the smallest and largest values of \(c\) for which the level curve still meets the constraint circle; as Figure 4.8.9 shows, at those values the two circles are tangent and the gradients are collinear, \(\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g\text{.}\)
🔗
We compute the gradients:
\begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= (2x-2)\,\hat{\imath} + (2y+2)\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \nabla g \amp= 2x\,\hat{\imath} + 2y\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
The Lagrange multiplier equation gives
\begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= \lambda\,\nabla g\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; (2x-2)\,\hat{\imath} + (2y+2)\,\hat{\jmath} = 2\lambda x\,\hat{\imath} + 2\lambda y\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; x - 1 = \lambda x, \quad y + 1 = \lambda y. \end{align*}
Adding the two equations yields
\begin{equation} x + y = \lambda(x + y).\tag{4.8.10} \end{equation}
This equation is satisfied if \(\lambda = 1\) or if \(x + y = 0\text{.}\) The case \(\lambda = 1\) is impossible, since substituting it into \(x - 1 = \lambda x\) gives \(-1 = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(y = -x\text{,}\) which we substitute into the constraint:
\begin{align*} x^2 + y^2 = 4 \amp\;\Rightarrow\; 2x^2 = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; x = -y = \pm\sqrt{2}. \end{align*}
🔗
Therefore the solutions are \((x,y) = (\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2)\) and \((x,y) = (-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2)\text{.}\) Using (4.8.9),
\begin{align*} f(\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2) \amp= 2(\sqrt 2 - 1)^2 + 3 = 9 - 4\sqrt 2\\ f(-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2) \amp= 2(\sqrt 2 + 1)^2 + 3 = 9 + 4\sqrt 2. \end{align*}
Since the circle is closed and bounded and \(f\) is continuous, these are the absolute extrema: \(f(\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2) = 9 - 4\sqrt 2\) is the absolute minimum and \(f(-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2) = 9 + 4\sqrt 2\) is the absolute maximum.
🔗
The answer agrees with the geometry of (4.8.9): on the constraint circle, \(f\) equals \(3\) plus the square of the distance to the point \((1,-1)\text{.}\) The point of the circle farthest from \((1,-1)\) is \((-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2)\text{,}\) at distance \(2 + \sqrt 2\text{,}\) giving \(f = 3 + (2+\sqrt 2)^2 = 9 + 4\sqrt 2\text{,}\) and the nearest point is \((\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2)\text{,}\) at distance \(2 - \sqrt 2\text{,}\) giving \(f = 3 + (2-\sqrt 2)^2 = 9 - 4\sqrt 2\text{.}\) Note also that in Figure 4.8.9 the gradients \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) point in the same direction at both extrema, but with different multipliers: from \(x - 1 = \lambda x\text{,}\) we get \(\lambda = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\) at the minimum and \(\lambda = 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\) at the maximum.

The completed-square form (4.8.9) shows that, with no constraint, \(f\) would take its least possible value \(3\) at the vertex \((1,-1)\)—the bottom of the paraboloid. But that vertex lies inside the constraint circle (a distance \(\sqrt 2 \lt 2\) from the origin), so it is not available to us here: the method of Lagrange multipliers instead locates the highest and lowest points of \(f\) along the circle. As the level circles of \(f\) expand outward from \((1,-1)\text{,}\) the first one to reach the constraint circle gives the constrained minimum \(9 - 4\sqrt 2\) and the last gives the maximum \(9 + 4\sqrt 2\text{;}\) at each of these contacts the two circles are tangent and \(\nabla f\) is parallel to \(\nabla g\text{.}\)
