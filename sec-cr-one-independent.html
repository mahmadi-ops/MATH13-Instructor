    Subsection 4.4.1 One Independent Variable

    Theorem 4.4.1. Chain Rule for Functions of Two Independent Variables.

    Consider the two-variable function \(w = f(x,y)\text{.}\) If the following conditions are satisfied:
    🔗
    1. \(w = f(x,y)\) has continuous partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\text{,}\)
    🔗
    2. \(x = x(t)\) and \(y = y(t)\) are differentiable functions of \(t\text{,}\)
    🔗
    then the function \(w = f(x(t), y(t))\) is a differentiable function of \(t\) and
    \begin{equation} \frac{dw}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}.\tag{4.4.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Figure 4.4.2 provides an easy way to remember the chain rule. Note that \(w\) is the dependent variable since it depends on \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) and \(x\) and \(y\) are known as the intermediate variables, whereas \(t\) is the independent variable. To use the diagram, read every path from \(w\) down to \(t\text{,}\) multiply the derivatives along each path, and add the results.
    🔗
    We now give a geometric intuition behind the chain rule (4.4.1): why it holds, and in particular why the two rates add, without ever finding an equation for the surface. Imagine a hiker walking over terrain whose elevation is \(w = f(x,y)\text{,}\) where \(x\) measures eastward position and \(y\) measures northward position. Both coordinates change with time, \(x = x(t)\) and \(y = y(t)\text{,}\) and we want to know how quickly the hiker’s elevation changes.
    🔗
    Why splitting the motion works. The total change in elevation depends only on the starting and ending positions, not on which route we imagine between them, so we may compute it in two stages:
    1. Move east. Over a very small eastward displacement \(\Delta x\text{,}\) the local eastward slope \(f_x\) gives an elevation change of approximately \(f_x\,\Delta x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Move north. Over a very small northward displacement \(\Delta y\text{,}\) the local northward slope \(f_y\) gives an additional elevation change of approximately \(f_y\,\Delta y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Together,
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\Delta w}_{\text{total}} \approx \underbrace{f_x\,\Delta x}_{\text{east}} + \underbrace{f_y\,\Delta y}_{\text{north}} . \end{equation*}
    For a tiny displacement the slopes vary very little. (The northward slope is really taken at \(B\) rather than at \(A\text{;}\) the continuity of \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) in Theorem 4.4.1 is what makes that difference negligible.) Dividing by the elapsed time \(\Delta t\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\Delta w}{\Delta t} \approx f_x\,\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} + f_y\,\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}, \end{equation*}
    and letting \(\Delta t \to 0\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dw}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}, \end{equation*}
    which is (4.4.1).
    🔗
    The central idea: each coordinate motion contributes to the rate of climbing, and those contributions add. A negative contribution means that component of the motion carries the hiker downhill.
    🔗
    We will gain a deeper understanding of this when we discuss directional derivatives, in Section 4.5, “Directional Derivatives and Gradient Vectors”.
    🔗
    The geometric argument above can be turned into a proof of Theorem 4.4.1. It uses the hypothesis that \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are continuous, and nothing more than the one-variable Mean Value Theorem, and it follows the hiker’s route \(A \to B \to C\) of Figure 4.4.3: east first, then north.
    🔗

    A proof, assuming continuous partial derivatives.
    Fix \(t_0\) and let \((a,b) = \bigl(x(t_0), y(t_0)\bigr)\text{.}\) For \(h \neq 0\) small, set
    \begin{equation*} \Delta x = x(t_0+h) - x(t_0), \qquad \Delta y = y(t_0+h) - y(t_0). \end{equation*}
    Since \(x\) and \(y\) are differentiable at \(t_0\text{,}\) they are continuous there, so \(\Delta x \to 0\) and \(\Delta y \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\text{.}\) Let \(D\) be an open disk centered at \((a,b)\) on which \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist, and take \(h\) small enough that \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\) is less than the radius of \(D\text{.}\) Name the corners of the route as in Figure 4.4.3:
    \begin{equation*} A = (a,b), \qquad B = (a + \Delta x,\, b), \qquad C = (a + \Delta x,\, b + \Delta y). \end{equation*}
    The two legs, the points \((a + s\,\Delta x,\, b)\) and \((a + \Delta x,\, b + s\,\Delta y)\) for \(0 \le s \le 1\text{,}\) then lie in \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: Move one variable at a time. Since \(w(t_0) = f(A)\) and \(w(t_0+h) = f(C)\text{,}\) adding and subtracting \(f(B)\text{,}\) the elevation at the corner, gives
    \begin{align*} \amp w(t_0+h) - w(t_0) = f(C) - f(A)\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\bigl[f(B) - f(A)\bigr]}_{\text{only } x \text{ changes}} + \underbrace{\bigl[f(C) - f(B)\bigr]}_{\text{only } y \text{ changes}}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Apply the Mean Value Theorem to each bracket. The function \(g(s) = f(s, b)\) is differentiable for \(s\) between \(a\) and \(a + \Delta x\text{,}\) with \(g'(s) = f_x(s, b)\text{.}\) By the Mean Value Theorem there is a \(\theta_1\) in \((0,1)\) with
    \begin{align*} \amp f(B) - f(A)\\ \amp \quad = f(a+\Delta x, b) - f(a,b)\\ \amp \quad = f_x(a + \theta_1\Delta x,\, b)\,\Delta x. \end{align*}
    (If \(\Delta x = 0\text{,}\) both sides are \(0\) and any \(\theta_1\) works.) Similarly, the Mean Value Theorem applied to \(s \mapsto f(a + \Delta x,\, s)\) gives a \(\theta_2\) in \((0,1)\) with
    \begin{align*} \amp f(C) - f(B)\\ \amp \quad = f(a+\Delta x, b+\Delta y) - f(a+\Delta x, b)\\ \amp \quad = f_y(a+\Delta x,\, b + \theta_2\Delta y)\,\Delta y. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Divide by \(h\text{.}\) Writing \(P_h = (a+\theta_1\Delta x,\, b)\) and \(Q_h = (a+\Delta x,\, b+\theta_2\Delta y)\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{w(t_0+h) - w(t_0)}{h} \amp = f_x(P_h)\,\frac{\Delta x}{h}\\ \amp \quad + f_y(Q_h)\,\frac{\Delta y}{h}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 4: Take the limit. Both \(P_h\) and \(Q_h\) are within distance \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\) of \((a,b)\text{,}\) and this distance tends to \(0\) as \(h \to 0\text{.}\) Hence \(P_h \to (a,b)\) and \(Q_h \to (a,b)\text{,}\) and by the continuity of \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) at \((a,b)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} f_x(P_h) \to f_x(a,b), \qquad f_y(Q_h) \to f_y(a,b). \end{equation*}
    (The numbers \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) depend on \(h\text{,}\) but this does not matter: only the location of \(P_h\) and \(Q_h\) is used.) Combined with \(\Delta x/h \to x'(t_0)\) and \(\Delta y/h \to y'(t_0)\text{,}\) the product and sum rules for limits give
    \begin{align*} \frac{dw}{dt}(t_0) \amp = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{w(t_0+h) - w(t_0)}{h}\\ \amp = f_x(a,b)\,x'(t_0) + f_y(a,b)\,y'(t_0), \end{align*}
    which is (4.4.1) at \(t = t_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The proof used less than Theorem 4.4.1 assumes: only that \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist near \((a,b)\) and are continuous at \((a,b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.5. How Fast Is the Hiker Climbing?

    At a particular instant, a hiker is walking on a hill whose elevation (in meters) is \(w = f(x,y)\text{,}\) with \(x\) and \(y\) measured in kilometers east and north, respectively. We do not know a formula for the hill, only its slopes at the hiker’s current position,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 40\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{km}}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 30\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{km}}. \end{equation*}
    At that same instant the hiker is moving east at \(3\) km/h and north at \(4\) km/h:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dx}{dt} = 3\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = 4\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}. \end{equation*}
    How quickly is the hiker’s elevation increasing, in meters per hour?

    Solution.
    By the chain rule (4.4.1),
    \begin{align*} \frac{dw}{dt} \amp = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}\\ \amp = \underbrace{(40)(3)}_{\text{east}} + \underbrace{(30)(4)}_{\text{north}}\\ \amp = 120 + 120 = 240\,\text{m/h}. \end{align*}
    The units work out: \(\frac{\text{m}}{\text{km}}\cdot\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{h}}\text{.}\) The hiker’s elevation is increasing at \(240\) meters per hour, and each component of the motion accounts for \(120\) m/h of this increase.
    🔗
    Keep the two speeds distinct. The hiker’s horizontal walking speed is \(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\) km/h, whereas the vertical climbing rate is \(240\) m/h. The question asks for the latter.

    Example 4.4.6. Pulling an Espresso Shot.

    As an espresso shot pours, coffee solids dissolve into the water and the cup fills. The strength of what is in the cup is its concentration
    \begin{equation*} C(m,V) = \frac{m}{V}, \end{equation*}
    where \(m\) is the mass of dissolved coffee solids in the cup (in grams) and \(V\) is the volume of liquid in the cup (in milliliters). Both change during the shot, so \(m = m(t)\) and \(V = V(t)\text{,}\) with \(t\) in seconds.
    🔗
    
    A photograph of an espresso shot pouring from a machine into a clear glass cup. A label pointing at the cup reads “\(m(t)\text{,}\) mass of dissolved solids (g)”, a second reads “\(V(t)\text{,}\) volume of liquid (mL)”, and a box on the cup asks “\(C(t) = {}\text{?}\)”.
    🔗
    At \(t = 20\) s,
    \begin{gather*} m = 4\,\text{g}, \quad V = 40\,\text{mL},\\ \frac{dm}{dt} = 0.12\,\text{g/s}, \quad \frac{dV}{dt} = 1.6\,\text{mL/s}. \end{gather*}
    At \(t = 20\) s, is the cup getting stronger or weaker, and how fast?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Dependence. \(C\) depends on \(m\) and \(V\text{,}\) and each of those depends on \(t\text{,}\) as the tree diagram below shows.
    🔗

    Step 2: Chain rule. There are two paths from \(C\) down to \(t\text{,}\) so (4.4.1), with \(C\) in place of \(w\text{,}\) has two terms:
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{\partial C}{\partial m}\frac{dm}{dt} + \frac{\partial C}{\partial V}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dm}{dt} - \frac{m}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Plug in. First, \(C = \frac{4}{40} = 0.10\,\text{g/mL}\text{,}\) about \(10\%\text{,}\) a typical espresso strength. Then
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{0.12}{40} - \frac{4}{40^2}\,(1.6)\\ \amp = 0.003 - 0.004\\ \amp = -0.001\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 4: Interpret. The concentration is decreasing by about \(0.001\) g/mL (\(0.1\) percentage point) per second. Solids are still going into the cup, but the cup is getting weaker.
    🔗
    Why? Write \(m' = \frac{dm}{dt}\) and \(V' = \frac{dV}{dt}\text{,}\) and factor the derivative:
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{1}{V}\bigl(m' - C\,V'\bigr)\\ \amp = \frac{V'}{V}\left(\frac{m'}{V'} - C\right). \end{align*}
    The ratio \(m'/V'\) is the concentration of the stream coming out of the machine right now. Since \(V'/V \gt 0\text{,}\) the cup gets weaker exactly when the stream is weaker than the cup:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dC}{dt} \lt 0 \iff \frac{m'}{V'} \lt C. \end{equation*}
    Here the stream is \(0.12/1.6 = 0.075\,\text{g/mL}\) (\(7.5\%\)), weaker than the \(10\%\) already in the cup, so adding it dilutes the shot. This is why baristas cut a shot once the stream turns pale.
    🔗
    
    The espresso photograph, now with the stream leaving the machine labelled “Incoming stream \(\frac{m'(t)}{V'(t)}\)”. Beside it, a tree diagram: \(C(m,V)\) at the top, arrows down to \(m(t)\) and \(V(t)\text{,}\) and from each an arrow down to \(t\text{.}\) Below the tree, the chain rule \(\frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial m}\frac{dm}{dt} + \frac{\partial C}{\partial V}\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dm}{dt} - \frac{m}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt}\text{.}\) Along the bottom, the interpretation: the term \(\frac{1}{V}\frac{dm}{dt}\) adds solids and makes the cup stronger, the term \(-\frac{m}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt}\) adds liquid and makes the cup weaker, and \(\frac{dC}{dt} \lt 0\) exactly when \(\frac{m'(t)}{V'(t)} \lt C(t)\text{.}\)

    Example 4.4.7. Pulling an Espresso Shot, Continued.

    Use the same data at \(t = 20\) s as in Example 4.4.6.
    🔗

    (a)

    What flow rate \(\frac{dV}{dt}\) would make the strength of the cup momentarily constant at \(t = 20\) s (with \(m\text{,}\) \(V\text{,}\) and \(\frac{dm}{dt}\) unchanged)? Interpret your answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Set \(\frac{dC}{dt} = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{V}\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{m}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt}, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{align*} \frac{dV}{dt} \amp = \frac{V}{m}\frac{dm}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{40}{4}\,(0.12) = 1.2\,\text{mL/s}. \end{align*}
    At \(1.2\) mL/s, the stream’s concentration is \(0.12/1.2 = 0.10\,\text{g/mL}\text{,}\) exactly the cup’s concentration. Adding liquid of the same strength leaves the strength unchanged. A faster flow dilutes the cup, and a slower flow strengthens it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use a linear approximation to estimate the concentration at \(t = 22\) s.
    🔗
    Solution.
    A linear approximation replaces the graph of \(C\) near \(t = 20\) by its tangent line there,
    \begin{align*} L(t) \amp = C(20) + \left.\frac{dC}{dt}\right|_{t=20}(t - 20)\\ \amp = 0.10 - 0.001\,(t - 20). \end{align*}
    Close to \(t = 20\) the curve and its tangent line are close, so \(C(22) \approx L(22)\text{.}\) With \(\Delta t = 2\) s,
    \begin{align*} C(22) \amp \approx C(20) + \left.\frac{dC}{dt}\right|_{t=20}\Delta t\\ \amp = 0.10 + (-0.001)(2)\\ \amp = 0.098\,\text{g/mL}, \end{align*}
    about \(9.8\%\text{.}\)

    (c)

    Explain, using the tree diagram, why the chain rule for \(\frac{dC}{dt}\) has two terms, and what each term means physically.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(C\) depends on \(t\) only through \(m\) and \(V\text{,}\) and the tree has two paths from \(C\) down to \(t\text{:}\) one through \(m\) and one through \(V\text{.}\) Each path contributes the product of the derivatives along its edges, and the total rate is the sum of the paths:
    🔗
    • Path through \(m\text{:}\)
      \begin{align*} \frac{\partial C}{\partial m}\frac{dm}{dt} \amp = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{0.12}{40}\\ \amp = +0.003\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}. \end{align*}
      New dissolved solids make the cup stronger.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Path through \(V\text{:}\)
      \begin{align*} \frac{\partial C}{\partial V}\frac{dV}{dt} \amp = -\frac{m}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp = -\frac{4}{1600}\,(1.6)\\ \amp = -0.004\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}. \end{align*}
      New liquid makes the cup weaker.
      🔗
      🔗
    The two effects compete. At \(t = 20\) s dilution wins, so \(\frac{dC}{dt} \lt 0\text{.}\)

    Theorem 4.4.8. Chain Rule for Functions of Three Independent Variables.

    Consider the three-variable function \(w = f(x,y,z)\text{.}\) If the following conditions are satisfied:
    🔗
    1. \(w = f(x,y,z)\) has continuous partial derivatives \(f_x\text{,}\) \(f_y\text{,}\) and \(f_z\text{,}\)
    🔗
    2. \(x = x(t)\text{,}\) \(y = y(t)\text{,}\) and \(z = z(t)\) are differentiable functions of \(t\text{,}\)
    🔗
    then the function \(w = f(x(t), y(t), z(t))\) is a differentiable function of \(t\) and
    \begin{equation} \frac{dw}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}.\tag{4.4.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Again the tree diagram of Figure 4.4.9 provides an easy way to remember the chain rule: now there are three paths from \(w\) down to \(t\text{,}\) so the formula has three terms.

    Example 4.4.10. Pulling an Americano.

    Some espresso machines make an Americano by adding hot water to the cup while the shot is still pouring. The cup now holds the espresso of Example 4.4.6 together with the added water, so its concentration is
    \begin{equation*} C(m,V,W) = \frac{m}{V + W}, \end{equation*}
    where \(m\) is the mass of dissolved coffee solids in the cup (in grams), \(V\) is the volume of espresso (in milliliters), and \(W\) is the volume of added hot water (in milliliters). All three change during the shot, so \(m = m(t)\text{,}\) \(V = V(t)\) and \(W = W(t)\text{,}\) with \(t\) in seconds. At \(t = 20\) s,
    \begin{align*} m = 4\,\text{g}, \amp \quad \frac{dm}{dt} = 0.12\,\text{g/s},\\ V = 40\,\text{mL}, \amp \quad \frac{dV}{dt} = 1.6\,\text{mL/s},\\ W = 40\,\text{mL}, \amp \quad \frac{dW}{dt} = 2\,\text{mL/s}. \end{align*}
    At \(t = 20\) s, is the cup getting stronger or weaker, and how fast?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: Dependence. \(C\) depends on \(m\text{,}\) \(V\) and \(W\text{,}\) and each of those depends on \(t\text{,}\) as the tree diagram below shows.

    Step 2: Chain rule. There are three paths from \(C\) down to \(t\text{,}\) so (4.4.2), with \(C\) in place of \(w\text{,}\) has three terms:
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{\partial C}{\partial m}\frac{dm}{dt} + \frac{\partial C}{\partial V}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp \quad + \frac{\partial C}{\partial W}\frac{dW}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{V+W}\frac{dm}{dt}\\ \amp \quad - \frac{m}{(V+W)^2}\frac{dV}{dt}\\ \amp \quad - \frac{m}{(V+W)^2}\frac{dW}{dt}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 3: Plug in. First, \(C = \frac{4}{40 + 40} = 0.05\,\text{g/mL}\text{,}\) about \(5\%\text{,}\) half the strength of the straight shot. Then
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{0.12}{80} - \frac{4}{80^2}\,(1.6)\\ \amp \quad - \frac{4}{80^2}\,(2)\\ \amp = 0.0015 - 0.001 - 0.00125\\ \amp = -0.00075\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 4: Interpret. The concentration is decreasing by about \(0.00075\) g/mL (\(0.075\) percentage point) per second. The solids path adds \(+0.0015\text{,}\) and the two dilution paths take away \(0.001\) (espresso) and \(0.00125\) (water), so dilution wins.
    🔗
    Why? As in Example 4.4.6, write \(m' = \frac{dm}{dt}\text{,}\) \(V' = \frac{dV}{dt}\text{,}\) \(W' = \frac{dW}{dt}\text{,}\) and factor the derivative:
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{m' - C\,(V' + W')}{V+W}\\ \amp = \frac{V'+W'}{V+W}\left(\frac{m'}{V'+W'} - C\right). \end{align*}
    What enters the cup now is espresso and water together, and \(\frac{m'}{V'+W'}\) is the concentration of that combined stream. Here it is \(0.12/3.6 \approx 0.033\,\text{g/mL}\) (\(3.3\%\)), weaker than the \(5\%\) already in the cup, so the cup gets weaker. The espresso stream by itself, at \(7.5\%\text{,}\) is stronger than the cup: it is the added water that tips the balance.

    Example 4.4.11. Pulling an Americano, Continued.

    Use the same data at \(t = 20\) s as in Example 4.4.10.
    🔗

    (a)

    At what rate \(\frac{dW}{dt}\) should the hot water flow to keep the strength of the cup momentarily constant at \(t = 20\) s (with everything else unchanged)? What happens if the water is turned off?
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the factored form, \(\frac{dC}{dt} = 0\) exactly when \(m' = C\,(V' + W')\text{,}\) that is,
    \begin{align*} V' + W' \amp = \frac{m'}{C} = \frac{0.12}{0.05}\\ \amp = 2.4\,\text{mL/s},\\ W' \amp = 2.4 - 1.6\\ \amp = 0.8\,\text{mL/s}. \end{align*}
    At that rate the combined stream is \(0.12/2.4 = 0.05\,\text{g/mL}\text{,}\) the cup’s own strength. Faster water dilutes the cup and slower water lets it strengthen. With the water off (\(W' = 0\)) only the \(7.5\%\) espresso stream comes in, stronger than the cup, and
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{1}{80}\bigl(0.12 - 0.05\,(1.6)\bigr)\\ \amp = +0.0005\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}, \end{align*}
    so the cup gets stronger.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In this example \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial W}\text{.}\) Explain why, and use it to redo the computation with the chain rule for two intermediate variables, (4.4.1).
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both partial derivatives equal \(-\frac{m}{(V+W)^2}\) because \(C\) depends on \(V\) and \(W\) only through the total volume \(U = V + W\text{:}\) the cup cannot tell espresso from tap water. So \(C = \frac{m}{U}\text{,}\) a function of the two intermediate variables \(m\) and \(U\text{,}\) with
    \begin{align*} U \amp = 40 + 40 = 80\,\text{mL},\\ \frac{dU}{dt} \amp = \frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{dW}{dt} = 3.6\,\text{mL/s}. \end{align*}
    Then (4.4.1) gives
    \begin{align*} \frac{dC}{dt} \amp = \frac{1}{U}\frac{dm}{dt} - \frac{m}{U^2}\frac{dU}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{0.12}{80} - \frac{4}{6400}\,(3.6)\\ \amp = 0.0015 - 0.00225\\ \amp = -0.00075\,(\text{g/mL})/\text{s}, \end{align*}
    the same answer: the \(V\) and \(W\) terms of the three-variable rule combine into the single \(U\) term.
