    1.

    (a)

    Compute the vector \(\vec v\) whose tail and tip are at \((2,0,-1)\) and \(\left(3,2,-1+\sqrt{11}\right)\text{,}\) respectively.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec v = \left\langle 3-2,\ 2-0,\ -1+\sqrt{11}-(-1)\right\rangle = \left\langle 1,\ 2,\ \sqrt{11}\right\rangle, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec v\| = \sqrt{1+4+11} = \sqrt{16} = 4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a vector \(\vec w\) that points in the opposite direction to \(\vec v\) (see part (a)) and whose length is triple the length of \(\vec v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Opposite direction and three times as long:
    \begin{equation*} \vec w = -3\vec v = \left\langle -3,\ -6,\ -3\sqrt{11}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    (check: \(\|\vec w\| = 3\cdot 4 = 12\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec v + \vec w\) (see part (b)).
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec v + \vec w = \vec v - 3\vec v = -2\vec v = \left\langle -2,\ -4,\ -2\sqrt{11}\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} \frac{\vec v + \vec w}{\|\vec v + \vec w\|} \amp = \frac{-2\vec v}{2\|\vec v\|} = -\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|} = -\frac14\left\langle 1,\ 2,\ \sqrt{11}\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac14,\ -\frac12,\ -\frac{\sqrt{11}}{4}\right\rangle. \end{align*}
    Note that \(\vec v + \vec w\) points opposite to \(\vec v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the two vectors \(\vec u = \langle 2,-1,3\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \langle 1,2,2\rangle\text{.}\) Write the vector \(\vec u\) as the sum of two vectors, one of which is parallel to \(\vec v\) and the other one is perpendicular to it.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\cdot\vec v = 2 - 2 + 6 = 6 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \|\vec v\|^2 = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{align*} \vec u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}\vec u \amp = \left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\|\vec v\|^2}\right)\vec v = \frac69\langle 1,2,2\rangle = \left\langle \frac23,\ \frac43,\ \frac43\right\rangle,\\ \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u - \vec u_{\parallel} \amp = \langle 2,-1,3\rangle - \left\langle \frac23,\ \frac43,\ \frac43\right\rangle = \left\langle \frac43,\ -\frac73,\ \frac53\right\rangle. \end{align*}
    Check: \(\vec u_{\perp}\cdot\vec v = \frac43 - \frac{14}{3} + \frac{10}{3} = 0\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\langle 2,-1,3\rangle}_{\vec u} = \underbrace{\left\langle \frac23,\ \frac43,\ \frac43\right\rangle}_{\vec u_{\parallel}} + \underbrace{\left\langle \frac43,\ -\frac73,\ \frac53\right\rangle}_{\vec u_{\perp}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Parametrize the following curves.
    🔗

    (a)

    The quarter-ellipse from \((0,3)\) to \((-2,0)\) centered at the origin.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The ellipse is \(\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{3}\right)^2 = 1\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = 2\cos t, \qquad y(t) = 3\sin t, \qquad \frac{\pi}{2} \le t \le \pi. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(t = \frac{\pi}{2}\) gives \((0,3)\) and \(t = \pi\) gives \((-2,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The circle of radius 3 centered at \((-1,2)\text{,}\) traced once clockwise starting at \((2,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x+1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 3^2\text{.}\) A minus sign on the sine term makes the motion clockwise:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = -1 + 3\cos t, \qquad y(t) = 2 - 3\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(t = 0\) gives \((2,2)\text{;}\) \(t = \frac{\pi}{2}\) gives \((-1,-1)\text{,}\) so the point moves down first (clockwise).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    The line segment from \((2,-1)\) to \((-1,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \vec r(t) \amp = \langle 2,-1\rangle + t\,\langle -1-2,\ 3-(-1)\rangle\\ \amp = \langle 2,-1\rangle + t\,\langle -3,4\rangle, \end{align*}
    i.e.
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = 2 - 3t, \qquad y(t) = -1 + 4t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 1. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(t = 0\) gives \((2,-1)\) and \(t = 1\) gives \((-1,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Consider an ellipse whose center is at the origin. Assume that it has foci \((0,-4)\) and \((0,4)\text{,}\) and suppose that the length of its minor axis is 6. Assume that the major axis is in the \(y\)-direction. What is the equation of this ellipse?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Foci on the \(y\)-axis: \(c = 4\text{.}\) Minor axis \(2b = 6\text{,}\) so \(b = 3\text{.}\) For an ellipse, \(a^2 = b^2 + c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\text{,}\) so \(a = 5\text{.}\) With the major axis vertical,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{b^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\frac{x^2}{3^2} + \frac{y^2}{5^2} = 1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Find a unit vector that is perpendicular to the \(xz\)-plane and points in the negative \(y\)-direction. If the tail of this vector is placed at \((2,-1,3)\text{,}\) where is its tip?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Perpendicular to the \(xz\)-plane means parallel to the \(y\)-axis: \(\langle 0,c,0\rangle\text{.}\) Length \(1\) and pointing in the negative \(y\)-direction gives \(c = -1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -\hat\jmath = \langle 0,-1,0\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Tip \(=\) tail \(+\) vector \(= (2,\ -1-1,\ 3) = (2,-2,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Mark the following statements as True or False. If a statement is true, prove it and if a statement is false, provide a counter-example.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) be any two vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\text{.}\) Then \(\|\vec u + \vec v\|^2 + \|\vec u - \vec v\|^2 = 2\|\vec u\|^2 + 2\|\vec v\|^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Use \(\|\vec a\|^2 = \vec a\cdot\vec a\) and the distributive and commutative properties of the dot product:
    \begin{align*} \|\vec u + \vec v\|^2 \amp = (\vec u + \vec v)\cdot(\vec u + \vec v) = \|\vec u\|^2 + 2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v + \|\vec v\|^2,\\ \|\vec u - \vec v\|^2 \amp = (\vec u - \vec v)\cdot(\vec u - \vec v) = \|\vec u\|^2 - 2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v + \|\vec v\|^2. \end{align*}
    Adding, the \(\pm 2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v\) terms cancel, leaving \(2\|\vec u\|^2 + 2\|\vec v\|^2\text{.}\) (Geometrically: the sum of the squares of the diagonals of a parallelogram equals the sum of the squares of its four sides.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Let \(\vec u\text{,}\) \(\vec v\text{,}\) and \(\vec w\) be non-zero vectors such that \(\vec u\cdot\vec v = \vec u\cdot\vec w\text{.}\) Then \(\vec v = \vec w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Counter-example: \(\vec u = \langle 1,0,0\rangle\text{,}\) \(\vec v = \langle 1,2,0\rangle\text{,}\) \(\vec w = \langle 1,0,5\rangle\text{.}\) Then \(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 1 = \vec u\cdot\vec w\text{,}\) but \(\vec v \ne \vec w\text{.}\) (In general \(\vec u\cdot(\vec v - \vec w) = 0\) only says \(\vec v - \vec w\) is perpendicular to \(\vec u\text{,}\) not that it is \(\vec 0\text{.}\))
    🔗

    7.

    Consider the points \(P(1,0,1)\text{,}\) \(Q(2,1,0)\text{,}\) and \(R(0,2,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Find an equation of the plane that contains \(P\text{,}\) \(Q\text{,}\) and \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 1,1,-1\rangle\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR} = \langle -1,2,2\rangle\text{.}\) A normal vector is
    \begin{align*} \vec n = \overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \hat\imath \amp \hat\jmath \amp \hat k \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ -1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (2+2)\,\hat\imath - (2-1)\,\hat\jmath + (2+1)\,\hat k = \langle 4,-1,3\rangle. \end{align*}
    Using the point \(P\text{:}\) \(4(x-1) - (y-0) + 3(z-1) = 0\text{,}\) i.e.
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{4x - y + 3z = 7}. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(Q\text{:}\) \(8 - 1 + 0 = 7\) \(\checkmark\text{;}\) \(R\text{:}\) \(0 - 2 + 9 = 7\) \(\checkmark\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find parametric equations of the line through \(S(5,2,5)\) that is perpendicular to the plane in part (a).
    🔗
    Solution.
    The line is parallel to the normal \(\vec n = \langle 4,-1,3\rangle\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = 5 + 4t, \qquad y(t) = 2 - t, \qquad z(t) = 5 + 3t, \qquad t \in \mathbb R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the point where the line in part (b) meets the plane, and use it to find the distance from \(S\) to the plane.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substitute into \(4x - y + 3z = 7\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 4(5+4t) - (2-t) + 3(5+3t) = 7 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 33 + 26t = 7 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = -1. \end{equation*}
    The line meets the plane at \(F = (5-4,\ 2+1,\ 5-3) = (1,3,2)\) (check: \(4 - 3 + 6 = 7\) \(\checkmark\)). The distance from \(S\) to the plane is
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\overrightarrow{FS}\right\| = \|\langle 4,-1,3\rangle\| = \sqrt{16 + 1 + 9} = \sqrt{26} \approx 5.10. \end{equation*}
    (Same answer from the projection formula: \(\dfrac{|\vec n\cdot\overrightarrow{PS}|}{\|\vec n\|} = \dfrac{|\langle 4,-1,3\rangle\cdot\langle 4,2,4\rangle|}{\sqrt{26}} = \dfrac{26}{\sqrt{26}} = \sqrt{26}\text{.}\))
