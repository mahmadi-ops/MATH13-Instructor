    Subsection 3.2.1 Definitions, Terminology, and Notation

    Some quantities can be described using real numbers alone, such as time or temperature. To describe other quantities we need a direction as well as a magnitude. Consider the velocity of a car: we need to know both the speed of the car and its direction of motion to specify its velocity completely. For instance, we might say that the car is moving with speed \(65\) miles per hour in the direction of north. Such quantities are referred to as vector quantities, and they are the subject of this section.
    🔗
    A vector is a directed line segment from an initial point \(A\) to a terminal point \(B\text{,}\) and is denoted by \(\overrightarrow{AB}\text{.}\) We denote the length of a vector by \(\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|\text{.}\) See Figure 3.2.1.
    🔗
    Two vectors are equal if they have the same direction and the same magnitude. Notice that this definition says nothing about where a vector is located: a vector may be moved around the plane freely, and as long as its direction and length are unchanged it is the same vector. The vectors shown in Figure 3.2.2 are all equal to each other, \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{CD} = \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{EF}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Only one of the vectors in Figure 3.2.2 starts from the origin. We say that \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) is in standard position, and we denote it by a bold letter such as \(\mathbf v = \overrightarrow{OP}\text{,}\) which is also referred to as the standard position vector.
    🔗
    In 2D, if the initial point of a vector is the origin, i.e. \((0,0)\text{,}\) and its final point is \((v_1,v_2)\text{,}\) then the component form of the vector is \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle\text{.}\) In 3D, if the initial point of a vector is the origin, i.e. \((0,0,0)\text{,}\) and its final point is \((v_1,v_2,v_3)\text{,}\) then the component form of the vector is \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In Figure 3.2.3 we have \(\mathbf v = \overrightarrow{PQ}\text{.}\) Also \(x_1 + v_1 = x_2\text{,}\) \(y_1 + v_2 = y_2\text{,}\) and \(z_1 + v_3 = z_2\text{.}\) This means we have
    \begin{equation} \mathbf v = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle = \langle x_2 - x_1,\; y_2 - y_1,\; z_2 - z_1 \rangle.\tag{3.2.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The magnitude of \(\mathbf v = \overrightarrow{PQ}\) is the same as the distance between the two points \(P(x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \(Q(x_2,y_2,z_2)\text{,}\) that is
    \begin{align} \|\mathbf v\| = \|\overrightarrow{PQ}\| \amp = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2}\notag\\ \amp = \sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2 + (y_1-y_2)^2 + (z_1-z_2)^2}.\tag{3.2.2} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 3.2.4. Length and Standard Position Vector.

    1. Compute the length of the vector with the initial point \(P(-1,0,1)\) and the final point \(Q(1,1,-1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the standard position vector corresponding to this vector.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. By (3.2.2), the length of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) is
    \begin{align*} \|\overrightarrow{PQ}\| \amp = \sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2 + (y_1-y_2)^2 + (z_1-z_2)^2}\\ \amp = \sqrt{(-1-1)^2 + (0-1)^2 + (1+1)^2} = 3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    B. By (3.2.1), the standard position vector is
    \begin{align*} \mathbf v \amp = \langle x_2 - x_1,\; y_2 - y_1,\; z_2 - z_1 \rangle\\ \amp = \langle 1-(-1),\; 1-0,\; -1-1 \rangle = \langle 2, 1, -2 \rangle. \end{align*}
    As a check, \(\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2} = 3\text{,}\) which agrees with part A, as it must, since \(\mathbf v\) and \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) are the same vector.
