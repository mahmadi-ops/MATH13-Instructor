    Subsection 4.5.3 Directions of Fastest Increase, Fastest Decrease, and No Change

    The directional derivative can be expressed as
    \begin{align} D_{\hat{u}} f \amp= \nabla f\cdot\hat{u}\notag\\ \amp= \|\nabla f\|\,\|\hat{u}\|\cos\theta\notag\\ \amp= \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta,\tag{4.5.11} \end{align}
    where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\nabla f\) and the unit vector \(\hat{u}\text{.}\) The first line is (4.5.5), obtained from the chain rule in Subsection 4.5.2; the second is the geometric form of the dot product; and the third uses \(\|\hat{u}\| = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Remark 4.5.16. Three Consequences of \(D_{\hat{u}} f = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\).

    Only the factor \(\cos\theta\) depends on the direction, and it runs over \([-1,1]\) as \(\hat{u}\) turns through the circle of directions at \(P_0\text{.}\) Its three extreme values single out three directions, drawn in Figure 4.5.17:
    🔗
    • When \(\nabla f\) and \(\hat{u}\) are in the same direction (\(\cos\theta = 1\)), \(f\) increases most rapidly and \(D_{\hat{u}} f = \|\nabla f\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(\nabla f\) and \(\hat{u}\) have opposite directions (\(\cos\theta = -1\)), \(f\) decreases most rapidly and \(D_{\hat{u}} f = -\|\nabla f\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(\nabla f\) and \(\hat{u}\) are orthogonal (\(\cos\theta = 0\)), \(f\) does not change, and \(D_{\hat{u}} f = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 4.5.3.1. Verifying the Three Special Directions.

    The three cases listed above are all consequences of a single formula, \(D_{\hat{u}} f = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\text{.}\) In this activity you will derive them yourself and then check your answers against Figure 4.5.18, where \(\hat{u}\) can be dragged around the circle of directions.
    🔗

    (a)

    Beginning with \(D_{\hat{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \hat{u}\text{,}\) explain why the dot product can be rewritten as \(\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    For any two vectors, \(\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b = \|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|\cos\theta\) with \(\theta\) the angle between them. Applying this to \(\nabla f\) and \(\hat{u}\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} D_{\hat{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \hat{u}\\ \amp= \|\nabla f\|\,\|\hat{u}\|\cos\theta\\ \amp= \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta , \end{align*}
    where the last step uses \(\|\hat{u}\| = 1\text{.}\) Without that assumption the factor \(\|\hat{u}\|\) would survive, and rescaling \(\hat{u}\) would change \(D_{\hat{u}} f\) without changing the direction being measured.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Treat \(\|\nabla f\|\) as fixed and let \(\theta\) run over \([0, 2\pi)\text{.}\) What are the largest and smallest values of \(D_{\hat{u}} f\text{,}\) and at which angles do they occur? For which angles is \(D_{\hat{u}} f = 0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Since \(-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1\text{,}\) the quantity \(\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\) ranges between \(-\|\nabla f\|\) and \(\|\nabla f\|\text{.}\) The largest value \(\|\nabla f\|\) occurs at \(\theta = 0\text{,}\) with \(\hat{u}\) pointing along \(\nabla f\text{;}\) the smallest, \(-\|\nabla f\|\text{,}\) occurs at \(\theta = \pi\text{,}\) with \(\hat{u}\) pointing along \(-\nabla f\text{.}\) The directional derivative vanishes when \(\cos\theta = 0\text{,}\) that is at \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(\theta = 3\pi/2\text{,}\) the two directions orthogonal to \(\nabla f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now take \(f(x,y) = 1 + x^2 + y^2\) at \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\) the function of Example 4.5.6. Compute \(\nabla f\) and \(\|\nabla f\|\) at \(P_0\text{,}\) write \(D_{\hat{u}} f\) as a function of \(\theta\text{,}\) and give the three special unit vectors explicitly.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Here \(\nabla f = 2x\,\hat{\imath} + 2y\,\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) so \((\nabla f)_{(1,1)} = 2\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}\) and \(\|\nabla f\| = 2\sqrt 2\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{gather*} D_{\hat{u}} f = 2\sqrt 2 \,\cos\theta . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    The fastest increase happens along \(\hat{u} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) giving \(D_{\hat{u}} f = 2\sqrt 2 \approx 2.83\text{,}\) which is the value computed in Example 4.5.6. The fastest decrease happens along \(-\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) giving \(-2\sqrt 2\text{.}\) There is no change along \(\pm\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}\right)\text{,}\) the two directions orthogonal to \(\nabla f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Check your work against the figure below. Drag \(\hat{u}\) around the circle and confirm that the meter agrees with \(2\sqrt 2\cos\theta\) at several angles. Then use the three buttons and compare the readings with your answers to the previous task. As you drag, at what angle does the reading change most quickly, and at what angle is it momentarily stationary?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The three buttons should give \(2\sqrt 2 \approx 2.83\text{,}\) \(-2\sqrt 2 \approx -2.83\text{,}\) and \(0\text{.}\) The reading changes fastest where \(\left|\frac{d}{d\theta}\cos\theta\right| = |\sin\theta|\) is largest, namely at \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(3\pi/2\text{,}\) the very directions where \(D_{\hat{u}} f\) itself is zero. It is momentarily stationary at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\text{,}\) where \(D_{\hat{u}} f\) is largest and smallest. So a small change of heading matters least when you are already pointing straight uphill, and most when you are moving along a level direction.

    Example 4.5.19. Example III.

    Consider the function \(f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{y^2}{2}\text{.}\) Find the directions in which, at the point \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\)
    🔗
    1. \(f\) increases most rapidly,
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(f\) decreases most rapidly,
      🔗
      🔗
    3. there is no change in \(f\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. As explained above, \(f\) increases most rapidly in the direction of \(\nabla f\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align} \nabla f \amp= x\,\hat{\imath} + y\,\hat{\jmath} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}\notag\\ \amp= \hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}.\tag{4.5.12} \end{align}
    🔗
    Note that we need to divide \(\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)} = \hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}\) by its length to find the unit vector corresponding to the direction of most rapid increase:
    \begin{equation} \frac{\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}}{\left\|\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}\right\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}.\tag{4.5.13} \end{equation}
    🔗
    B. For the direction of most rapid decrease, we take the opposite direction:
    \begin{equation} -\frac{\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}}{\left\|\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}\right\|} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}.\tag{4.5.14} \end{equation}
    🔗
    C. To find the directions of zero change in \(f\text{,}\) we require
    \begin{align} \left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}\cdot\hat{u} \amp= (\hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath})\cdot(u_1\hat{\imath} + u_2\hat{\jmath})\notag\\ \amp= u_1 + u_2\notag\\ \amp= 0 \quad\text{or}\quad u_1\notag\\ \amp= -u_2,\tag{4.5.15} \end{align}
    and since \(\hat{u}\) is a unit vector, we have
    \begin{equation} \|\hat{u}\|^2 = u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1.\tag{4.5.16} \end{equation}
    🔗
    After substituting (4.5.15) in (4.5.16), we conclude
    \begin{equation} 2u_1^2 = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad u_1 = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}.\tag{4.5.17} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Hence, the directions of zero change in \(f\) at \(P_0(1,1)\) are
    \begin{align} \hat{u} \amp= \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tilde{\hat{u}}\notag\\ \amp= -\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}.\tag{4.5.18} \end{align}
    🔗
    Note that \(\tilde{\hat{u}} = -\hat{u}\text{.}\) All four directions are shown in Figure 4.5.20: the directions of zero change are tangent to the level curve of \(f\) through \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\) while the directions of most rapid increase and decrease are perpendicular to it.
