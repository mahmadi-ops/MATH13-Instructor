    Example 4.4.12. The Temperature Experienced by a Moving Ant.

    Suppose that the temperature on a metal plate is given by the function \(T\) with
    \begin{equation*} T(x,y) = 100 - (x^2 + 4y^2), \end{equation*}
    where the temperature is measured in degrees Fahrenheit and \(x\) and \(y\) are each measured in feet. Now suppose that an ant is walking on the metal plate in such a way that it walks in a straight line from the point \((1,4)\) to the point \((5,6)\text{.}\)
    1. Find parametric equations \((x(t), y(t))\) for the ant’s coordinates as it walks the line from \((1,4)\) to \((5,6)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What can you say about \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) for every value of \(t\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Determine the instantaneous rate of change in temperature with respect to \(t\) that the ant is experiencing at the moment it is halfway from \((1,4)\) to \((5,6)\text{,}\) using your parametric equations for \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\) Include units on your answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    a. The slope of the line is
    \begin{equation*} \text{slope} = \frac{6-4}{5-1} = \frac{1}{2}, \end{equation*}
    so the line is \(y - 4 = \frac12 (x-1)\text{.}\) Choosing \(x - 1 = t\text{,}\) that is \(x = t+1\text{,}\) gives
    \begin{equation} x(t) = t+1, \qquad y(t) = \frac{t}{2} + 4, \qquad 0 \le t \le 4.\tag{4.4.4} \end{equation}
    At \(t = 0\) the ant is at \((1,4)\) and at \(t = 4\) it is at \((5,6)\text{,}\) as required.
    🔗
    b. Differentiating (4.4.4),
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dx}{dt} = 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    for every value of \(t\text{.}\) Both are constant, which reflects the fact that the ant walks along a straight line at a steady rate.
    🔗
    c. The tree diagram for this composition is the one in Figure 4.4.2, with \(T\) in place of \(w\text{.}\) By (4.4.1),
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} = (-2x)(1) + (-8y)\left(\frac12\right) = -2x - 4y. \end{equation*}
    The halfway point is
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{5+1}{2},\; \frac{6+4}{2}\right) = (3,5), \end{equation*}
    and therefore
    \begin{align*} \left.\frac{dT}{dt}\right|_{(3,5)} \amp = (-2)(3) - (4)(5) = -26\\ \amp \text{degrees Fahrenheit per unit time}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Alternatively, \(\frac{dT}{dt}\) can be written in terms of \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dT}{dt} = -2(t+1) - 4\left(\frac{t}{2}+4\right) = -4t - 18. \end{equation*}
    Also notice that at \(t = 2\) the ant is at \((3,5)\text{,}\) and hence
    \begin{equation*} \left.\frac{dT}{dt}\right|_{t=2} = (-4)(2) - 18 = -26 \text{ degrees Fahrenheit per unit time}, \end{equation*}
    in agreement with the first computation. The temperature is falling as the ant walks, which makes sense: it is moving away from the origin, where \(T\) is largest.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.13. Proving an Identity with the Chain Rule.

    Suppose that \(w = f(xz, yz)\text{,}\) where \(f\) is a differentiable function. Show that
    \begin{equation*} x\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} = z\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let us name the intermediate variables
    \begin{equation*} u = xz \qquad \text{and} \qquad v = yz, \end{equation*}
    so that \(w = f(u,v)\) with \(u\) and \(v\) each depending on \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) The tree diagram is shown in Figure 4.4.14.
    🔗
    Reading the branches that lead down to \(z\text{,}\) and using \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = x\) and \(\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = y\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\,x + \frac{\partial w}{\partial v}\,y.\tag{4.4.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Only one branch leads down to \(x\text{,}\) namely the one through \(u\text{,}\) and \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = z\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\,z \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial w}{\partial u} = \frac{1}{z}\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}.\tag{4.4.6} \end{equation}
    Similarly, only one branch leads down to \(y\text{,}\) through \(v\text{,}\) and \(\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = z\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial v}\,z \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial w}{\partial v} = \frac{1}{z}\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}.\tag{4.4.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Substituting (4.4.6) and (4.4.7) into (4.4.5) gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = \frac{1}{z}\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\,x + \frac{1}{z}\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\,y, \end{equation*}
    and multiplying both sides by \(z\) yields
    \begin{equation*} z\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = x\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}, \end{equation*}
    which is the identity we wanted to prove.
