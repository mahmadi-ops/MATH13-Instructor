    1. 6 points.

    Evaluate each of the following limits, or show that the limit does not exist.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{xy + y^3}{x^2 + y^2}\) (3 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The limit does not exist. Let \(f(x,y) = \dfrac{xy + y^3}{x^2 + y^2}\) and approach the origin along two different paths.
    🔗
    Along the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\text{,}\) \(y \to 0\)):
    \begin{equation*} f(0,y) = \frac{0 + y^3}{0 + y^2} = y \;\longrightarrow\; 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Along the line \(y = x\) (\(x \to 0\)):
    \begin{equation*} f(x,x) = \frac{x^2 + x^3}{x^2 + x^2} = \frac{x^2(1+x)}{2x^2} = \frac{1+x}{2} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac12. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The two paths give two different values (\(0 \ne \frac12\)), so by the two-path test the limit does not exist.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x^2 - xy}{x^2 - y^2}\) (3 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substituting \((1,1)\) gives \(\frac00\text{,}\) so we simplify first. The function is defined where \(x^2 \ne y^2\text{,}\) that is, away from the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\text{.}\) Factor the numerator and the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2 - xy}{x^2 - y^2} = \frac{x\,(x-y)}{(x-y)(x+y)} = \frac{x}{x+y} \end{equation*}
    at every point of the domain, since \(x - y \ne 0\) there. The function \(\dfrac{x}{x+y}\) is continuous at \((1,1)\text{,}\) because its denominator is \(2 \ne 0\) there, so
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x^2 - xy}{x^2 - y^2} = \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x}{x+y} = \frac{1}{1+1} = \boxed{\frac12}. \end{equation*}

    2. 4 points.

    Let \(z = e^{x^2 y}\text{,}\) where \(x(u,v) = \sqrt{uv}\) and \(y(u,v) = \dfrac{1}{v}\text{.}\) Compute \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your final answer must be fully simplified.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the chain rule,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}. \end{equation*}
    The four partial derivatives are
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \amp = 2xy\,e^{x^2 y}, \amp \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \amp = x^2 e^{x^2 y},\\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \amp = \frac{u}{2\sqrt{uv}}, \amp \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} \amp = -\frac{1}{v^2}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \left(2xy\,e^{x^2 y}\right)\frac{u}{2\sqrt{uv}} + \left(x^2 e^{x^2 y}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{v^2}\right). \end{equation*}
    Now write everything in terms of \(u\) and \(v\text{.}\) Since \(x^2 y = uv\cdot\frac1v = u\text{,}\) we have \(e^{x^2 y} = e^u\text{,}\) and
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \amp = \left(2\sqrt{uv}\cdot\frac1v\cdot e^u\right)\frac{u}{2\sqrt{uv}} - \frac{uv}{v^2}\,e^u\\ \amp = \frac{u}{v}\,e^u - \frac{u}{v}\,e^u = \boxed{0}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Check: \(x^2 y = u\text{,}\) so \(z = e^u\) does not depend on \(v\) at all, which agrees with \(\partial z/\partial v = 0\text{.}\) (An answer that is correct but left unsimplified, for example \(\frac{u}{v}e^u - \frac{uv}{v^2}e^u\text{,}\) is not complete.)

    3. 5 points.

    Find the directional derivative of \(f(x,y) = x^2 y + y^2\) at the point \((-1,-1)\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\hat{u} = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The direction. The direction \(\hat{u} = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\) is given, and it is already a unit vector: \(\|\hat{u}\| = \sqrt{\frac12 + \frac12} = 1\text{.}\) So no normalization is needed.
    🔗
    The gradient. The partial derivatives are
    \begin{equation*} f_x = 2xy, \qquad f_y = x^2 + 2y. \end{equation*}
    At \(P_0(-1,-1)\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} f_x(-1,-1) \amp = 2(-1)(-1) = 2, \qquad f_y(-1,-1) = 1 - 2 = -1,\\ \text{so}\quad \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0} \amp = \langle 2, -1\rangle. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The directional derivative.
    \begin{align*} \left(D_{\hat{u}} f\right)_{P_0} \amp = \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0}\cdot\hat{u} = \langle 2,-1\rangle\cdot\left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\\ \amp = \frac{2-1}{\sqrt2} = \boxed{\frac{1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. 3 points.

    Identify and sketch the surface corresponding to the following equation:
    \begin{equation*} z^2 + y^2 - x^2 + 1 = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the equation in standard form:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = 1. \end{equation*}
    One squared term is positive and two are negative, and the right-hand side is \(1\text{.}\) Look at the traces:
    • \(z = 0\text{:}\) \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\text{,}\) a hyperbola in the \(xy\)-plane opening along the \(x\)-axis;
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = 0\text{:}\) \(x^2 - z^2 = 1\text{,}\) a hyperbola in the \(xz\)-plane opening along the \(x\)-axis;
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x = c\text{:}\) \(y^2 + z^2 = c^2 - 1\text{.}\) This is empty when \(|c| \lt 1\text{,}\) a single point when \(c = \pm 1\text{,}\) and a circle of radius \(\sqrt{c^2 - 1}\) when \(|c| \gt 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    There are no points of the surface with \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\text{,}\) so the surface comes in two separate pieces. It is a hyperboloid of two sheets whose axis is the \(x\)-axis, with vertices \((\pm 1, 0, 0)\text{.}\) Its cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are circles.

    5. 2 points.

    Decide whether the statement below is true or false. Clearly justify your answer: a correct answer with an incorrect or unclear justification will not earn credit.
    🔗
    True or False? There exists a function \(f(x,y)\) such that
    \begin{equation*} f_x(x,y) = y \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_y(x,y) = x^2 \end{equation*}
    at every point \((x,y)\) of the plane.
    🔗
    (Assume that \(f\) and its partial derivatives \(f_x\text{,}\) \(f_y\text{,}\) \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are defined and continuous at every point of the \(xy\)-plane.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Suppose such a function \(f\) existed. Then
    \begin{equation*} f_{xy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(f_x\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y) = 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{yx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(f_y\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(x^2\right) = 2x. \end{equation*}
    By assumption \(f\text{,}\) \(f_x\text{,}\) \(f_y\text{,}\) \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are defined and continuous at every point of the plane, so the Mixed Derivative Theorem (Clairaut’s Theorem, Theorem 4.3.14) applies at every point \((a,b)\) and gives
    \begin{equation*} f_{xy}(a,b) = f_{yx}(a,b), \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad 1 = 2a \quad\text{for every } a. \end{equation*}
    This is false (for example, at the origin it says \(1 = 0\)). The contradiction shows that no such function exists.

    6. Bonus, 4 points.

    Consider the two planes
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal P_1:\ x + 2y + 2z = 3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathcal P_2:\ 2x - 2y + z = 3, \end{equation*}
    and let \(L\) be their line of intersection.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the dot product to show that the two planes are perpendicular. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The coefficients of \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) \(z\) give the normal vectors \(\vec n_1 = \langle 1,2,2\rangle\) and \(\vec n_2 = \langle 2,-2,1\rangle\text{.}\) Two planes are perpendicular exactly when their normal vectors are perpendicular, and
    \begin{equation*} \vec n_1\cdot\vec n_2 = (1)(2) + (2)(-2) + (2)(1) = 2 - 4 + 2 = 0, \end{equation*}
    so \(\vec n_1 \perp \vec n_2\) and the planes are perpendicular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the cross product to find a vector that is parallel to the line \(L\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The line \(L\) lies in both planes, so its direction is perpendicular to both normal vectors. Such a vector is
    \begin{align*} \vec n_1\times\vec n_2 \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \hat\imath \amp \hat\jmath \amp \hat k \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp -2 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} = (2+4)\,\hat\imath - (1-4)\,\hat\jmath + (-2-4)\,\hat k\\ \amp = \langle 6,3,-6\rangle = 3\,\langle 2,1,-2\rangle. \end{align*}
    Any non-zero multiple works; we use \(\vec d = \langle 2,1,-2\rangle\) (check: \(\vec d\cdot\vec n_1 = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0\) and \(\vec d\cdot\vec n_2 = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    The point \(A(1,0,1)\) lies on both planes. Find parametric equations of \(L\text{,}\) and an equation of the plane through the origin that is perpendicular to \(L\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The line \(L\) passes through \(A(1,0,1)\) and is parallel to \(\vec d = \langle 2,1,-2\rangle\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{x(t) = 1 + 2t, \quad y(t) = t, \quad z(t) = 1 - 2t, \quad t \in \mathbb R.} \end{equation*}
    (Check: \((1+2t) + 2t + 2(1-2t) = 3\) and \(2(1+2t) - 2t + (1-2t) = 3\) for every \(t\text{.}\))
    🔗
    A plane perpendicular to \(L\) has the direction vector of \(L\) as its normal vector, so \(\vec n = \langle 2,1,-2\rangle\text{.}\) Passing through the origin,
    \begin{equation*} 2(x-0) + 1(y-0) - 2(z-0) = 0, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \boxed{2x + y - 2z = 0.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Bonus, 2 points.

    A hyperbola has vertices \((-2,0)\) and \((2,0)\text{,}\) and its asymptotes are the lines \(y = \pm\frac32 x\text{.}\) Find the equation of the hyperbola and its foci.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertices lie on the \(x\)-axis and the center is the origin, so the hyperbola opens left and right and has the form
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \end{equation*}
    with vertices \((\pm a, 0)\text{,}\) asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x\text{,}\) and foci \((\pm c, 0)\) where \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\text{.}\) The vertices give \(a = 2\text{,}\) and the asymptotes give \(\frac{b}{a} = \frac32\text{,}\) so \(b = \frac32\cdot 2 = 3\text{.}\) The hyperbola is
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1} \qquad \left(\text{equivalently } 9x^2 - 4y^2 = 36\right). \end{equation*}
    For the foci, \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 4 + 9 = 13\text{,}\) so \(c = \sqrt{13}\) and the foci are
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\left(-\sqrt{13}, 0\right) \text{ and } \left(\sqrt{13}, 0\right)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
