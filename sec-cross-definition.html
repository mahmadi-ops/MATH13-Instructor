    Subsection 3.4.1 Definition and Properties of the Cross Product

    Definition 3.4.1. The Cross Product (First Definition).

    Given two vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) in \(\mathbb R^3\text{,}\) the cross product \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\) is the vector defined by
    \begin{equation} \mathbf u \times \mathbf v = \left(\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta\right)\hat{n},\tag{3.4.1} \end{equation}
    where \(\theta\text{,}\) with \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{,}\) is the angle between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{,}\) and \(\hat{n}\) is the unit vector orthogonal to both \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) whose direction is given by the right-hand rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    The magnitude of \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\) is equal to the area of the parallelogram built on the two vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 3.4.2.
    🔗

    Fact 3.4.3. Area of the Parallelogram.

    \begin{equation} \text{Area of the parallelogram} = \|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\||\sin\theta| = \|\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\|.\tag{3.4.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that \(\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\||\sin\theta|\) is the length of the cross product \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\text{.}\) Since \(\sin(\theta)\) can be negative, we need to include its absolute value.
    🔗
    The vector \(\hat{n}\) is the unit vector that determines the direction of \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\) using the right-hand rule. Note that \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\) is orthogonal (perpendicular) to both vectors, as shown in Figure 3.4.4.
    🔗

    Fact 3.4.6. Parallel Vectors.

    From (3.4.1), it follows that the cross product of two parallel vectors is zero.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \mathbf u \parallel \mathbf v \iff \mathbf u \times \mathbf v = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf u, \mathbf v \neq \mathbf 0.\tag{3.4.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.4.7. Properties of the Cross Product.

    If \(\mathbf u\text{,}\) \(\mathbf v\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf w\) are any vectors and \(r\text{,}\) \(s\) are scalars, then
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle (r\mathbf u) \times (s\mathbf v) = (rs)(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \mathbf u \times (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) = \mathbf u \times \mathbf v + \mathbf u \times \mathbf w\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) \times \mathbf u = \mathbf v \times \mathbf u + \mathbf w \times \mathbf u\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \mathbf v \times \mathbf u = -(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \mathbf 0 \times \mathbf u = \mathbf 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Although the cross product was defined geometrically above, all five properties are most easily checked using the equivalent component formula (3.4.4) established in the next section: if \(\mathbf a = \langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf b = \langle b_1,b_2,b_3\rangle\text{,}\) then \(\mathbf a \times \mathbf b = \langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\; a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\; a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle\text{.}\) Write \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1,u_2,u_3\rangle\text{,}\) \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf w = \langle w_1,w_2,w_3\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (1) Each component of \((r\mathbf u)\times(s\mathbf v)\) has the form \((ru_i)(sv_j) - (ru_j)(sv_i) = rs(u_iv_j-u_jv_i)\text{,}\) which is \(rs\) times the corresponding component of \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\text{.}\) Hence \((r\mathbf u)\times(s\mathbf v) = (rs)(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (2) The first component of \(\mathbf u \times (\mathbf v + \mathbf w)\) is
    \begin{equation*} u_2(v_3+w_3) - u_3(v_2+w_2) = (u_2v_3-u_3v_2) + (u_2w_3-u_3w_2), \end{equation*}
    which is the sum of the first components of \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf w\text{.}\) The same holds for the other two components, so \(\mathbf u \times (\mathbf v+\mathbf w) = \mathbf u \times \mathbf v + \mathbf u \times \mathbf w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (3) By the same computation with the roles of the two factors exchanged, the first component of \((\mathbf v+\mathbf w)\times \mathbf u\) is \((v_2+w_2)u_3 - (v_3+w_3)u_2 = (v_2u_3-v_3u_2) + (w_2u_3-w_3u_2)\text{,}\) the sum of the first components of \(\mathbf v \times \mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf w \times \mathbf u\text{;}\) likewise for the other two components, so \((\mathbf v+\mathbf w)\times \mathbf u = \mathbf v \times \mathbf u + \mathbf w \times \mathbf u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (4) The first component of \(\mathbf v \times \mathbf u\) is \(v_2u_3 - v_3u_2 = -(u_2v_3-u_3v_2)\text{,}\) the negative of the first component of \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\text{;}\) likewise for the other two components, so \(\mathbf v \times \mathbf u = -(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (5) Since \(\mathbf 0 = \langle 0,0,0\rangle\text{,}\) every component of \(\mathbf 0 \times \mathbf u\) is a difference of products each containing a factor of \(0\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf 0 \times \mathbf u = \mathbf 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4.8.

    Show that \((\mathbf u - \mathbf v) \times (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = 2(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the distributive properties of the cross product from Theorem 3.4.7,
    \begin{equation*} (\mathbf u - \mathbf v) \times (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = \mathbf u \times \mathbf u + \mathbf u \times \mathbf v - \mathbf v \times \mathbf u - \mathbf v \times \mathbf v. \end{equation*}
    Since a vector is parallel to itself, \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf u = \mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf v \times \mathbf v = \mathbf 0\) by Fact 3.4.6. Also \(\mathbf v \times \mathbf u = -(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\) by Theorem 3.4.7, so
    \begin{equation*} (\mathbf u - \mathbf v) \times (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = \mathbf 0 + \mathbf u \times \mathbf v - \bigl(-(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v)\bigr) - \mathbf 0 = 2(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.9. Computing a cross product using the first definition.

    Use Definition 3.4.1 to compute
    🔗
    1. \(\hat{\imath} \times \hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) and also \(\hat{\jmath} \times \hat{\imath}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf u = 4\hat{\imath}\) and \(\mathbf v\) is a vector of length \(3\) lying in the \(xy\)-plane, making an angle of \(30^\circ\) with \(\mathbf u\) measured counterclockwise.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Part 1. The vectors \(\hat{\imath}\) and \(\hat{\jmath}\) are unit vectors, so \(\|\hat{\imath}\| = \|\hat{\jmath}\| = 1\text{,}\) and they are perpendicular, so \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and \(\sin\theta = 1\text{.}\) Both lie in the \(xy\)-plane, so the unit vector orthogonal to both is either \(\hat{k}\) or \(-\hat{k}\text{;}\) curling the fingers of the right hand from \(\hat{\imath}\) toward \(\hat{\jmath}\) points the thumb along \(+\hat{k}\text{,}\) so \(\hat{n} = \hat{k}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \hat{\imath} \times \hat{\jmath} = \left(\|\hat{\imath}\|\|\hat{\jmath}\|\sin 90^\circ\right)\hat{k} = (1)(1)(1)\,\hat{k} = \hat{k}. \end{equation*}
    For \(\hat{\jmath} \times \hat{\imath}\) the two lengths and the angle are unchanged, but the right-hand rule now runs from \(\hat{\jmath}\) toward \(\hat{\imath}\text{,}\) which points the thumb along \(-\hat{k}\text{.}\) Hence \(\hat{\jmath} \times \hat{\imath} = -\hat{k} = -(\hat{\imath} \times \hat{\jmath})\text{,}\) in agreement with property 4 of Theorem 3.4.7.
    🔗
    Part 2. Here \(\|\mathbf u\| = 4\text{,}\) \(\|\mathbf v\| = 3\text{,}\) and \(\theta = 30^\circ\text{,}\) so the length of the cross product is
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\| = \|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\sin 30^\circ = (4)(3)\left(\tfrac12\right) = 6. \end{equation*}
    Both vectors lie in the \(xy\)-plane, and turning from \(\mathbf u\) to \(\mathbf v\) is counterclockwise, so the right-hand rule again gives \(\hat{n} = \hat{k}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf u \times \mathbf v = 6\,\hat{k} = \langle 0,0,6\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As a check on the magnitude, Fact 3.4.3 says \(\|\mathbf u \times \mathbf v\|\) is the area of the parallelogram built on \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{:}\) its base is \(\|\mathbf u\| = 4\) and its height is \(\|\mathbf v\|\sin 30^\circ = 1.5\text{,}\) giving an area of \(6\text{.}\)
