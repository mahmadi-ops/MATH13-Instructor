    1. 3 points.

    Let \(P(1,-2,3)\) and \(Q\left(3,2,3+\sqrt5\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Compute the vector \(\vec v = \overrightarrow{PQ}\) and its length \(\|\vec v\|\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Tip minus tail:
    \begin{align*} \vec v \amp = \left\langle 3-1,\ 2-(-2),\ \left(3+\sqrt5\right)-3\right\rangle = \left\langle 2,\ 4,\ \sqrt5\right\rangle,\\ \|\vec v\| \amp = \sqrt{4+16+5} = \sqrt{25} = 5. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a vector \(\vec w\) that points in the opposite direction to \(\vec v\) and whose length is 10. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Opposite direction and twice as long as \(\vec v\) (since \(\|\vec v\| = 5\)):
    \begin{equation*} \vec w = -10\,\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|} = -2\vec v = \left\langle -4,\ -8,\ -2\sqrt5\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    (check: \(\|\vec w\| = 2\cdot 5 = 10\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec v + \vec w\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\vec v + \vec w = \vec v - 2\vec v = -\vec v = \left\langle -2,\ -4,\ -\sqrt5\right\rangle\text{,}\) which has length \(\|\vec v\| = 5\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{align*} \frac{\vec v + \vec w}{\|\vec v + \vec w\|} \amp = \frac{-\vec v}{\|\vec v\|} = -\frac15\left\langle 2,\ 4,\ \sqrt5\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac25,\ -\frac45,\ -\frac{\sqrt5}{5}\right\rangle. \end{align*}
    It is the unit vector pointing opposite to \(\vec v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. 2 points.

    Consider the surface with equation
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 4x + 6y = 3. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Identify this surface and describe it precisely. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Complete the squares in \(x\) and in \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \left(x^2 - 4x + 4\right) + \left(y^2 + 6y + 9\right) + z^2 \amp = 3 + 4 + 9\\ (x-2)^2 + (y+3)^2 + z^2 \amp = 16 = 4^2. \end{align*}
    Comparing with \((x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2 + (z-z_0)^2 = r^2\text{,}\) the surface is a sphere, with center \(C(2,-3,0)\) and radius \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Describe geometrically the set of all points \((x,y,z)\) that satisfy both \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 4x + 6y \le 3\) and \(y = -3\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By part (a) the inequality is \((x-2)^2 + (y+3)^2 + z^2 \le 16\text{:}\) the solid ball of radius \(4\) centered at \(C(2,-3,0)\) (the sphere together with its interior). Setting \(y = -3\) in it gives
    \begin{equation*} (x-2)^2 + z^2 \le 16, \qquad y = -3, \end{equation*}
    the solid disk of radius \(4\) centered at \(C(2,-3,0)\) and lying in the plane \(y = -3\text{.}\) (The plane \(y = -3\) passes through the center of the ball, so the slice is a disk of the full radius \(4\text{.}\) Because of the inequality, the set is the disk and not just its boundary circle.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. 2 points.

    Consider the two vectors \(\vec u = \langle 1,-1,3\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \langle 2,2,1\rangle\text{.}\) Write the vector \(\vec u\) as the sum of two vectors, one of which is parallel to \(\vec v\) and the other one is perpendicular to it.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\cdot\vec v = 2 - 2 + 3 = 3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \|\vec v\|^2 = 4 + 4 + 1 = 9. \end{equation*}
    The parallel part is the projection of \(\vec u\) onto \(\vec v\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}\vec u = \left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\|\vec v\|^2}\right)\vec v = \frac39\langle 2,2,1\rangle = \left\langle \frac23,\ \frac23,\ \frac13\right\rangle, \end{equation*}
    and the perpendicular part is what is left over:
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u - \vec u_{\parallel} = \langle 1,-1,3\rangle - \left\langle \frac23,\ \frac23,\ \frac13\right\rangle = \left\langle \frac13,\ -\frac53,\ \frac83\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(\vec u_{\perp}\cdot\vec v = \frac23 - \frac{10}{3} + \frac83 = 0\text{,}\) so \(\vec u_{\perp}\) really is perpendicular to \(\vec v\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{\langle 1,-1,3\rangle}_{\vec u} = \underbrace{\left\langle \frac23,\ \frac23,\ \frac13\right\rangle}_{\vec u_{\parallel}} + \underbrace{\left\langle \frac13,\ -\frac53,\ \frac83\right\rangle}_{\vec u_{\perp}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    4. 3 points.

    In parts (a) and (b), parametrize the given curve: give the parametric equations and the range of the parameter.
    🔗

    (a)

    The lower half of the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{16} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1\text{,}\) traced from \((4,0)\) to \((-4,0)\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The ellipse is \(\left(\frac{x}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{3}\right)^2 = 1\text{,}\) so \(x = 4\cos t\text{,}\) \(y = \pm 3\sin t\text{.}\) To start at \((4,0)\) and move below the \(x\)-axis we need \(y \le 0\) as \(t\) increases from \(0\text{,}\) so we take the minus sign:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = 4\cos t, \qquad y(t) = -3\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le \pi. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(t = 0\) gives \((4,0)\text{;}\) \(t = \frac{\pi}{2}\) gives \((0,-3)\text{,}\) below the axis; \(t = \pi\) gives \((-4,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The circle of radius 2 centered at \((-3,1)\text{,}\) traced once counterclockwise, starting at the point \((-3,3)\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The circle is \((x+3)^2 + (y-1)^2 = 2^2\text{.}\) The starting point \((-3,3)\) is the top of the circle, and moving counterclockwise from the top means moving to the left first. One parametrization that does this is
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = -3 - 2\sin t, \qquad y(t) = 1 + 2\cos t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \end{equation*}
    Check: \((x+3)^2 + (y-1)^2 = 4\sin^2 t + 4\cos^2 t = 4\text{;}\) \(t = 0\) gives \((-3,3)\text{;}\) \(t = \frac{\pi}{2}\) gives \((-5,1)\text{,}\) the leftmost point, so the motion is counterclockwise. (Equivalently, \(x = -3 + 2\cos t\text{,}\) \(y = 1 + 2\sin t\) for \(\frac{\pi}{2} \le t \le \frac{5\pi}{2}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Consider the curve with parametric equations
    \begin{equation*} x = \sqrt t, \qquad y = 1 - t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 4. \end{equation*}
    Sketch the curve. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From \(x = \sqrt t\) we get \(t = x^2\text{,}\) and also \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) since a square root is never negative. Substituting into \(y = 1 - t\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = 1 - x^2. \end{equation*}
    As \(t\) runs from \(0\) to \(4\text{,}\) \(x = \sqrt t\) runs from \(0\) to \(2\text{,}\) so the curve is only the part of the parabola \(y = 1 - x^2\) with \(0 \le x \le 2\text{:}\) it starts at \((0,1)\) when \(t = 0\text{,}\) passes through \((1,0)\) when \(t = 1\text{,}\) and ends at \((2,-3)\) when \(t = 4\text{,}\) moving to the right and downward as \(t\) increases. (The left half of the parabola is not traced at all, because \(x = \sqrt t\) is never negative, and the part with \(x \gt 2\) is not traced because \(t \le 4\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    5. 3 points.

    (a)

    Find the equation of the hyperbola whose vertices are \((0,-3)\) and \((0,3)\) and whose foci are \((0,-5)\) and \((0,5)\text{.}\) Find the equations of its asymptotes, and sketch the hyperbola together with its asymptotes. (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertices and foci lie on the \(y\)-axis, so the hyperbola opens up and down and has the form
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{x^2}{a^2} = 1, \end{equation*}
    with vertices \((0,\pm b)\) and foci \((0,\pm c)\text{,}\) where \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\text{.}\) The vertices give \(b = 3\) and the foci give \(c = 5\text{,}\) so \(a^2 = c^2 - b^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\) and \(a = 4\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\frac{y^2}{9} - \frac{x^2}{16} = 1} \qquad \left(\text{equivalently } 16y^2 - 9x^2 = 144\right). \end{equation*}
    The asymptotes are the lines \(y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x\text{,}\) exactly as for a hyperbola that opens left and right, that is,
    \begin{equation*} y = \frac34 x \qquad\text{and}\qquad y = -\frac34 x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the equation of the parabola whose focus is \((-3,0)\) and whose directrix is the line \(x = 3\text{.}\) In which direction does the parabola open? (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A point \((x,y)\) lies on the parabola exactly when its distance to the focus equals its distance to the directrix:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(x+3)^2 + y^2} = |x-3|. \end{equation*}
    Squaring both sides and expanding,
    \begin{align*} (x+3)^2 + y^2 \amp = (x-3)^2\\ x^2 + 6x + 9 + y^2 \amp = x^2 - 6x + 9, \end{align*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{y^2 = -12x}. \end{equation*}
    The directrix is vertical and the focus lies to its left, so the parabola opens to the left. (Its vertex is the origin, halfway between the focus and the directrix, and \(y^2 = 4px\) with \(p = -3\text{.}\))

    6. 4 points.

    Consider the points \(P(1,1,0)\text{,}\) \(Q(3,0,0)\text{,}\) \(R(1,3,2)\text{,}\) and \(S(5,5,-3)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Find an equation of the plane that contains \(P\text{,}\) \(Q\text{,}\) and \(R\text{,}\) and find the area of the triangle \(PQR\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 2,-1,0\rangle\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR} = \langle 0,2,2\rangle\text{.}\) A normal vector to the plane is
    \begin{align*} \vec n = \overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \hat\imath \amp \hat\jmath \amp \hat k \\ 2 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (-2-0)\,\hat\imath - (4-0)\,\hat\jmath + (4-0)\,\hat k = \langle -2,-4,4\rangle. \end{align*}
    Any non-zero multiple of \(\vec n\) is also a normal vector, so we may use the simpler \(\langle 1,2,-2\rangle = -\frac12\vec n\text{.}\) Using the point \(P\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 1(x-1) + 2(y-1) - 2(z-0) = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \boxed{x + 2y - 2z = 3}. \end{equation*}
    Check: \(Q\text{:}\) \(3 + 0 - 0 = 3\) \(\checkmark\text{;}\) \(R\text{:}\) \(1 + 6 - 4 = 3\) \(\checkmark\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The area of the triangle is half the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \text{Area} \amp = \frac12\left\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}\right\| = \frac12\|\langle -2,-4,4\rangle\|\\ \amp = \frac12\sqrt{4+16+16} = \frac12\cdot 6 = 3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find parametric equations of the line through \(S\) that is perpendicular to the plane of part (a). (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The line is parallel to the normal vector \(\langle 1,2,-2\rangle\) of the plane and passes through \(S(5,5,-3)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = 5 + t, \qquad y(t) = 5 + 2t, \qquad z(t) = -3 - 2t, \qquad t \in \mathbb R. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the point where the line of part (b) meets the plane, and use it to find the distance from \(S\) to the plane. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substitute the line into \(x + 2y - 2z = 3\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} (5+t) + 2(5+2t) - 2(-3-2t) \amp = 3\\ 21 + 9t \amp = 3\\ t \amp = -2. \end{align*}
    The line meets the plane at \(F = (5-2,\ 5-4,\ -3+4) = (3,1,1)\) (check: \(3 + 2 - 2 = 3\) \(\checkmark\)). Since the line is perpendicular to the plane, \(F\) is the point of the plane closest to \(S\text{,}\) and the distance from \(S\) to the plane is
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\overrightarrow{FS}\right\| = \|\langle 2,4,-4\rangle\| = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 16} = \sqrt{36} = 6. \end{equation*}
    (Same answer from the distance formula: \(\left|\overrightarrow{PS}\cdot\vec n\right| / \|\vec n\|\) with \(\vec n = \langle 1,2,-2\rangle\) and \(\overrightarrow{PS} = \langle 4,4,-3\rangle\) gives \(|4 + 8 + 6|/3 = 18/3 = 6\text{.}\))

    7. 3 points.

    Decide whether each statement below is true or false. Clearly justify your answer: a correct answer with an incorrect or unclear justification will not earn credit.
    🔗

    (a)

    True or False? Let \(\vec u\text{,}\) \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\) be vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) with \(\vec u \ne \vec 0\text{.}\) If \(\vec u\times\vec v = \vec u\times\vec w\) and \(\vec u\cdot\vec v = \vec u\cdot\vec w\text{,}\) then \(\vec v = \vec w\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Let \(\vec d = \vec v - \vec w\text{.}\) By the distributive properties of the two products,
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\times\vec d = \vec u\times\vec v - \vec u\times\vec w = \vec 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \vec u\cdot\vec d = \vec u\cdot\vec v - \vec u\cdot\vec w = 0. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\vec u \ne \vec 0\text{,}\) the first equation says that \(\vec d\) is parallel to \(\vec u\) or is \(\vec 0\text{;}\) either way \(\vec d = k\,\vec u\) for some scalar \(k\text{.}\) Substituting into the second equation,
    \begin{equation*} 0 = \vec u\cdot(k\,\vec u) = k\,(\vec u\cdot\vec u) = k\,\|\vec u\|^2, \end{equation*}
    and \(\|\vec u\| \ne 0\) forces \(k = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(\vec d = \vec 0\text{,}\) that is, \(\vec v = \vec w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Neither condition alone is enough. Adding to \(\vec w\) a vector perpendicular to \(\vec u\) does not change \(\vec u\cdot\vec w\text{,}\) and adding a vector parallel to \(\vec u\) does not change \(\vec u\times\vec w\text{;}\) a vector that is both perpendicular and parallel to \(\vec u \ne \vec 0\) can only be \(\vec 0\text{,}\) which is what the proof shows.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    True or False? Suppose \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are vectors with \(\|\vec u\| = 2\text{,}\) \(\|\vec v\| = 5\) and \(\|\vec u\times\vec v\| = 5\text{.}\) Then \(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 5\sqrt3\text{.}\) (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Let \(\theta\) be the angle between the two vectors, \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{.}\) From \(\|\vec u\times\vec v\| = \|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\sin\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} 5 = (2)(5)\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \frac12, \end{equation*}
    so \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}\) or \(\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}\text{.}\) The given information does not determine the sign of \(\cos\theta = \pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\text{,}\) and therefore only
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\cdot\vec v = \|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\cos\theta = \pm 5\sqrt3 \end{equation*}
    can be concluded: the absolute value \(|\vec u\cdot\vec v| = 5\sqrt3\) is determined, the sign is not.
    🔗
    Counterexample: take \(\vec u = \langle 2,0,0\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \left\langle -\frac{5\sqrt3}{2},\ \frac52,\ 0\right\rangle\text{,}\) the vector of length \(5\) that makes the angle \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\) with \(\vec u\text{.}\) Then \(\|\vec u\| = 2\text{,}\) \(\|\vec v\| = \sqrt{\frac{75}{4} + \frac{25}{4}} = 5\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\times\vec v = \left\langle 0-0,\ 0-0,\ 2\cdot\frac52 - 0\right\rangle = \langle 0,0,5\rangle, \qquad \|\vec u\times\vec v\| = 5, \end{equation*}
    but \(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 2\cdot\left(-\frac{5\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -5\sqrt3 \ne 5\sqrt3\text{.}\)
