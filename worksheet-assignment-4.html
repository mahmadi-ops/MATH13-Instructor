    1.

    1. Find an equation of the set of all points equidistant from the points \(A(-1,5,3)\) and \(B(6,2,-2)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe the geometrical shape corresponding to the set.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We want to find all points \(P(x,y,z)\) that have the same distance from \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) Squaring both distances (which is equivalent, since distances are non-negative) gives
      \begin{equation*} (x+1)^2 + (y-5)^2 + (z-3)^2 = (x-6)^2 + (y-2)^2 + (z+2)^2. \end{equation*}
      Expanding both sides,
      \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + 2x + 1 + y^2 - 10y + 25 + z^2 - 6z + 9\\ \amp = x^2 - 12x + 36 + y^2 - 4y + 4 + z^2 + 4z + 4,\\ \amp 2x - 10y - 6z + 35 = -12x - 4y + 4z + 44, \end{align*}
      and collecting terms,
      \begin{equation*} 14x - 6y - 10z = 9. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This is the equation of a plane. Its normal vector is \(\langle 14, -6, -10\rangle = 2\langle 7, -3, -5\rangle\text{,}\) which is parallel to \(\vec{AB} = \langle 6-(-1),\, 2-5,\, -2-3\rangle = \langle 7, -3, -5\rangle\text{.}\) Moreover, the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} C\left(\frac{-1+6}{2},\, \frac{5+2}{2},\, \frac{3+(-2)}{2}\right) = C\left(\frac52, \frac72, \frac12\right), \end{equation*}
      satisfies the equation, since \(14\left(\frac52\right) - 6\left(\frac72\right) - 10\left(\frac12\right) = 35 - 21 - 5 = 9\text{.}\) Hence the set is the plane that passes through the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\) and is orthogonal to \(\vec{AB}\text{,}\) i.e. the perpendicular bisector plane of the segment \(\overline{AB}\text{.}\) See Figure 5.6.7.
      🔗
      🔗

    2.

    Describe the following regions and graphs.
    1. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 4\text{,}\) \(z \ge 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 4\text{,}\) \(z = 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(z \ge 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(z = 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The key is to notice whether the first condition is an inequality (a solid region) or an equation (a surface), and then what the second condition does to it.
    1. The solid ball of radius \(2\) centered at the origin, restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{:}\) the upper half of the solid ball, including the flat disk in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Setting \(z = 0\) in the solid ball gives \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\text{:}\) the solid disk of radius \(2\) centered at the origin, lying in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The sphere of radius \(2\) restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{:}\) the upper hemisphere (a surface only), together with the circle where it meets the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Setting \(z = 0\) on the sphere gives \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{:}\) the circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗

    3.

    Consider the point \((3,7,-5)\text{.}\)
    1. What is its distance to the \(yz\)-plane?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is its distance to the \(y\)-axis?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The \(yz\)-plane is the plane \(x = 0\text{.}\) The closest point of that plane to \((3,7,-5)\) is obtained by simply setting the \(x\)-coordinate to zero, giving \((0,7,-5)\text{.}\) Hence the distance is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(3-0)^2 + (7-7)^2 + (-5+5)^2} = |3| = 3 . \end{equation*}
    In general the distance from \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) to the \(yz\)-plane is \(|x_0|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The \(y\)-axis consists of the points \((0,y,0)\text{.}\) The closest such point to \((3,7,-5)\) is the one with the same \(y\)-coordinate, namely \((0,7,0)\text{,}\) so the distance is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(3-0)^2 + (7-7)^2 + (-5-0)^2} = \sqrt{9+25} = \sqrt{34} . \end{equation*}
    In general the distance from \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) to the \(y\)-axis is \(\sqrt{x_0^2+z_0^2}\text{:}\) we drop the coordinate along the axis and keep the other two. See Figure 5.6.8.

    4. A Solid Bounded by a Paraboloid, a Plane, and a Cylinder.

    Consider the solid region bounded below by the paraboloid \(z = 1-x^2-y^2\text{,}\) above by the plane \(z=1\text{,}\) and on the outside by the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=1\text{.}\) Describe this solid.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The paraboloid \(z=1-x^2-y^2\) opens downward from its vertex \((0,0,1)\text{,}\) reaching \(z=0\) exactly on the circle \(x^2+y^2=1\text{,}\) the same circle where the cylinder meets the \(xy\)-plane. The plane \(z=1\) is tangent to the paraboloid at its vertex.
    🔗
    The solid consists of the points inside the cylinder that lie above the paraboloid and below the plane:
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2 \le 1, \qquad 1-x^2-y^2 \le z \le 1. \end{equation*}
    At the center \((x,y)=(0,0)\text{,}\) this range for \(z\) collapses to the single point \(z=1\text{,}\) since the paraboloid already reaches the plane there. At the rim \(x^2+y^2=1\text{,}\) the range is the full interval \(0 \le z \le 1\text{.}\) The solid is therefore a disk-shaped shell that thins to a single point at its center, as though a dome-shaped cavity bounded by the paraboloid had been hollowed out from the top of the solid cylinder \(x^2+y^2\le1\text{,}\) \(0\le z\le 1\text{,}\) shown in Figure 5.6.9.

    6.

    Find a vector of length 3 pointing in the opposite direction from the vector \(2\,\mathbf i - 4\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The given vector has length \(\sqrt{4 + 16 + 16} = \sqrt{36} = 6\text{,}\) so the unit vector in its direction is \(\frac16\langle 2, -4, 4\rangle = \left\langle \frac13, -\frac23, \frac23\right\rangle\text{.}\) Multiplying by \(-3\) reverses the direction and gives length \(3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -3\left\langle \frac13, -\frac23, \frac23\right\rangle = \langle -1, 2, -2\rangle = -\mathbf i + 2\,\mathbf j - 2\,\mathbf k. \end{equation*}

    7.

    Let \(\vec v\) be the vector that goes from the point \(\left(5,-4,\sqrt3\right)\) to the point \((3,-1,0)\text{.}\) Find a vector of length \(\tfrac13\) that points in the same direction as \(\vec v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vector from \(P(5,-4,\sqrt3)\) to \(Q(3,-1,0)\) is obtained by subtracting the tail from the head:
    \begin{equation*} \vec v = \vec{PQ} = \left\langle 3-5,\ -1-(-4),\ 0-\sqrt3 \right\rangle = \left\langle -2,\ 3,\ -\sqrt3 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Its length is
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec v\| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 3^2 + \left(-\sqrt3\right)^2} = \sqrt{4+9+3} = \sqrt{16} = 4 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The unit vector in the direction of \(\vec v\) is therefore
    \begin{equation*} \hat u = \frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|} = \frac14 \left\langle -2,\ 3,\ -\sqrt3 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Scaling by \(\tfrac13\) gives a vector of length \(\tfrac13\) pointing the same way:
    \begin{equation*} \frac13\,\hat u = \frac{1}{12}\left\langle -2,\ 3,\ -\sqrt3 \right\rangle = \left\langle -\frac16,\ \frac14,\ -\frac{\sqrt3}{12} \right\rangle . \end{equation*}

    8. A parallelogram in space.

    Consider the four points
    \begin{equation*} A(1,2,5), \qquad B(4,3,3), \qquad C(6,7,4), \qquad D(3,6,6). \end{equation*}
    1. Show that \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the point \(M\) at which the two diagonals of \(ABCD\) meet.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the perimeter of \(ABCD\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. A quadrilateral is a parallelogram exactly when one pair of opposite sides is given by the same vector, since equal vectors have both the same direction and the same length. Along the sides \(AB\) and \(DC\text{,}\)
      \begin{align*} \vec{AB} \amp = \langle 4-1,\; 3-2,\; 3-5 \rangle = \langle 3,\; 1,\; -2 \rangle,\\ \vec{DC} \amp = \langle 6-3,\; 7-6,\; 4-6 \rangle = \langle 3,\; 1,\; -2 \rangle. \end{align*}
      Since \(\vec{AB} = \vec{DC}\text{,}\) these two sides are parallel and have equal length, so \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram. The other pair of sides matches as well:
      \begin{equation*} \vec{AD} = \langle 2,\; 4,\; 1 \rangle = \vec{BC}. \end{equation*}
      See Figure 5.6.11.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AC\text{.}\) Using position vectors and \(\vec{AC} = \langle 5,\; 5,\; -1 \rangle\text{,}\)
      \begin{align*} \vec{OM} \amp = \vec{OA} + \tfrac12 \vec{AC} = \langle 1,\; 2,\; 5 \rangle + \tfrac12\langle 5,\; 5,\; -1 \rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle \tfrac72,\; \tfrac92,\; \tfrac92 \right\rangle. \end{align*}
      Travelling along the other diagonal instead, with \(\vec{BD} = \langle -1,\; 3,\; 3\rangle\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} \vec{OB} + \tfrac12 \vec{BD} = \langle 4,\; 3,\; 3 \rangle + \tfrac12\langle -1,\; 3,\; 3 \rangle = \left\langle \tfrac72,\; \tfrac92,\; \tfrac92 \right\rangle, \end{equation*}
      the same point, which confirms both the answer and part A. Hence \(M\left(\frac72,\, \frac92,\, \frac92\right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Opposite sides have equal length, so the perimeter is \(2\left(\left\|\vec{AB}\right\| + \left\|\vec{AD}\right\|\right)\text{,}\) where
      \begin{align*} \left\|\vec{AB}\right\| \amp = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{14},\\ \left\|\vec{AD}\right\| \amp = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{21}. \end{align*}
      The perimeter is therefore \(2\left(\sqrt{14} + \sqrt{21}\right) \approx 16.65\text{.}\) Since \(\sqrt{14} \ne \sqrt{21}\text{,}\) this parallelogram is not a rhombus.
      🔗
      🔗

    9.

    Consider the two vectors \(\mathbf u = 3\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k\) and \(\mathbf v = \mathbf i + \mathbf j\text{.}\) Write \(\mathbf u\) as the sum of a vector parallel to \(\mathbf v\) and a vector orthogonal to \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(\vec u \cdot \vec v = 3\) and \(\|\vec v\| = \sqrt{1+1} = \sqrt2\text{.}\) The component of \(\vec u\) parallel to \(\vec v\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v} \vec u = \left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{\|\vec v\|^2}\right)\vec v = \frac32\,(\mathbf i + \mathbf j) = \frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j, \end{equation*}
    and the component orthogonal to \(\vec v\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u - \vec u_{\parallel} = \left(3\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k\right) - \left(\frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j\right) = -\frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k. \end{equation*}
    Here \(\vec u_{\parallel}\) is parallel to \(\vec v\) and \(\vec u_{\perp}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec v\text{.}\) Note that \(\vec u_{\parallel} + \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u\text{.}\)
    🔗

    10.

    Determine whether the following pairs of vectors are orthogonal, parallel, or neither.
    1. \(\vec u_1 = -3\,\mathbf i + 9\,\mathbf j + 6\,\mathbf k\) and \(\vec u_2 = 4\,\mathbf i - 12\,\mathbf j - 8\,\mathbf k\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\vec v_1 = \mathbf i + \mathbf j - 2\,\mathbf k\) and \(\vec v_2 = \mathbf i + \mathbf j + \mathbf k\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\vec w_1 = a\,\mathbf i + b\,\mathbf j\) and \(\vec w_2 = c\,\mathbf k\text{,}\) where \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are non-zero real numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Parallel, since \(\vec u_2 = -\frac43\,\vec u_1\text{:}\) indeed \(-\frac43\langle -3, 9, 6\rangle = \langle 4, -12, -8\rangle\text{.}\) (Two vectors are parallel exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other.)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Orthogonal, since \(\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Orthogonal, since \(\vec w_1 \cdot \vec w_2 = \langle a, b, 0\rangle \cdot \langle 0, 0, c\rangle = 0\text{.}\) They are not parallel, because \(\vec w_2\) has a non-zero \(\mathbf k\) component while \(\vec w_1\) does not.

    11.

    A 100-meter dash is run on a track in the direction of the vector \(2\,\mathbf i + 6\,\mathbf j\text{.}\) The wind velocity is \(\mathbf v = 5\,\mathbf i + 2\,\mathbf j\) km/hr. The rules say that a legal wind speed measured in the direction of the dash must not exceed 5 km/hr.
    1. Will the race results be disqualified due to an illegal wind? Justify your answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the velocity vector of the wind in the direction of the dash.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec d = \langle 2, 6\rangle\) be the direction of the dash and \(\vec v = \langle 5, 2\rangle\) the wind velocity. Then
    \begin{equation*} \vec v \cdot \vec d = 10 + 12 = 22, \qquad \|\vec d\| = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}. \end{equation*}
    1. The wind speed measured in the direction of the dash is the length of the projection of \(\vec v\) onto \(\vec d\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \left\|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec d}\vec v\right\| = \frac{\vec v \cdot \vec d}{\|\vec d\|} = \frac{22}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{11}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 3.48 \text{ km/hr}. \end{equation*}
      Since \(3.48 \lt 5\text{,}\) the wind is legal and the race results will not be disqualified.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The velocity vector of the wind in the direction of the dash is the vector projection
      \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec d}\vec v \amp = \left(\frac{\vec v \cdot \vec d}{\|\vec d\|^2}\right)\vec d = \frac{22}{40}\langle 2, 6\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle \frac{11}{10}, \frac{33}{10}\right\rangle = 1.1\,\mathbf i + 3.3\,\mathbf j \text{ km/hr}. \end{align*}
      As a check, its length is \(\sqrt{1.1^2 + 3.3^2} = 1.1\sqrt{10} = \frac{11}{\sqrt{10}}\text{,}\) matching part A.

    12.

    A constant force is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \left\langle 2, -1, 3 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    A particle is constrained to move in directions parallel to the plane
    \begin{equation*} 2x - y + 2z = 0 . \end{equation*}
    For a displacement of one unit, the work done by the force is \(W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf d\) is an allowed unit direction lying in the plane.
    1. Find the unit direction in the plane that maximizes the work.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the maximum possible work per unit distance.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find one unit direction in the plane for which the work is zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reading the coefficients of the plane, a normal vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf n = \left\langle 2, -1, 2 \right\rangle , \qquad \|\mathbf n\| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3 . \end{equation*}
    A vector is parallel to the plane exactly when it is orthogonal to \(\mathbf n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Decompose the force into a normal piece and a piece lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel} . \end{equation*}
    The first piece comes from the projection formula. Since
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = 2(2) + (-1)(-1) + 3(2)\\ \amp = 4 + 1 + 6 = 11 , \end{align*}
    and \(\|\mathbf n\|^2 = 9\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \frac{11}{9}\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Subtracting,
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F_{\parallel} \amp = \mathbf F - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle - \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac49,\ \frac29,\ \frac59\right\rangle = \frac19\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    As a check, this really is parallel to the plane: \(\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = -8 - 2 + 10 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let \(\mathbf d\) be any allowed unit direction. Because \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F\) is orthogonal to every vector lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = \left(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel}\right)\cdot\mathbf d = \mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d . \end{equation*}
    The normal part of the force is simply wasted: it presses the particle against the constraint and does no work. Writing \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d = \|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\varphi = \|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\|\cos\varphi\text{,}\) the largest value occurs when \(\cos\varphi = 1\text{,}\) that is when \(\mathbf d\) points in the same direction as \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right\| = \sqrt{16+4+25} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 , \end{equation*}
    the maximizing unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_{\max} = \frac{\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} . \end{equation*}
    See Figure 5.6.12.
    🔗
    (b) The maximum work per unit displacement is the length of the in-plane component,
    \begin{align*} W_{\max} \amp = \left\|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\right\| = \left|\frac19\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right|\\ \amp = \frac19\left(3\sqrt5\right) = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745 . \end{align*}
    We can confirm this directly from the original force:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_{\max} \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot \frac{\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{-8-2+15}{3\sqrt5}\\ \amp = \frac{5}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} . \end{align*}
    Compare this with \(\|\mathbf F\| = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74\text{:}\) the constraint costs us about \(80\%\) of the force.
    🔗
    (c) We want a unit vector \(\mathbf d = \left\langle x,y,z\right\rangle\) satisfying two conditions at once: it must lie in the plane, and it must do no work. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf F = 0 , \end{equation*}
    or explicitly
    \begin{align*} 2x - y + 2z \amp = 0 ,\\ 2x - y + 3z \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    Subtracting the first equation from the second gives \(z = 0\text{,}\) and then \(2x - y = 0\text{,}\) so \(y = 2x\text{.}\) Taking \(x = 1\) gives the non-zero vector \(\left\langle 1,2,0\right\rangle\text{,}\) whose length is \(\sqrt{1+4} = \sqrt5\text{.}\) Hence one suitable unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left\langle 1,\ 2,\ 0\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Indeed \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2 - 2 + 0) = 0\text{,}\) and the opposite direction \(-\mathbf d_0\) works equally well. Geometrically, \(\mathbf d_0\) is the direction in the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{:}\) moving along it, the particle travels neither with nor against the usable part of the force.
