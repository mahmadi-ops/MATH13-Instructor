    Subsection 3.3.1 The Dot Product and the Angle between Two Vectors

    Consider two vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{,}\) and let \(\mathbf w = \mathbf u - \mathbf v\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 3.3.1. By the law of cosines,
    \begin{equation} \|\mathbf w\|^2 = \|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2 - 2\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.\tag{3.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.
    Watch the video below for a proof of the law of cosines using vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Writing \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3\rangle\text{,}\) note that \(\mathbf w = \mathbf u - \mathbf v\text{,}\) and hence
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf w\|^2 \amp = \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|^2 \\ \amp = (u_1-v_1)^2 + (u_2-v_2)^2 + (u_3-v_3)^2 \\ \amp = (u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2) + (v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2) \\ \amp \qquad - 2(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3). \end{aligned}\tag{3.3.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Also, from the law of cosines in (3.3.1) we have
    \begin{equation} \|\mathbf w\|^2 = (u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2) + (v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2) - 2\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.\tag{3.3.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    From (3.3.2) and (3.3.3), it follows that \(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 = \|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta\text{,}\) and therefore the angle between the two vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) can be evaluated using the following equation.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.3.3. Angle between Two Vectors.

    Let \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3\rangle\) be two nonzero vectors, and let \(\theta\text{,}\) with \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{,}\) be the angle between them. Then
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \cos\theta \amp = \frac{u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} \\ \iff\quad \theta \amp = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\right). \end{aligned}\tag{3.3.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.4. Dot Product.

    The term \(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3\) in the numerator of (3.3.4) is known as the dot product of the two vectors \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1,u_2,u_3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle\text{,}\) and is denoted \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3.\tag{3.3.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.5. Computing a dot product.

    Let \(\mathbf u = \langle 1,2,3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf v = \langle -2,0,4\rangle\text{.}\) Compute \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v\text{,}\) and use it to find the angle between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Multiply the corresponding components and add:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = (1)(-2) + (2)(0) + (3)(4) = -2 + 0 + 12 = 10. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since the dot product is positive, the angle between the two vectors is acute. To find it, use (3.3.4):
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf u\| \amp = \sqrt{1+4+9} = \sqrt{14} \\ \|\mathbf v\| \amp = \sqrt{4+0+16} = \sqrt{20} \\ \cos\theta \amp = \frac{\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{14}\,\sqrt{20}} \approx 0.5976 \\ \theta \amp = \cos^{-1}(0.5976) \approx 0.930 \text{ radians} \approx 53.3^{\circ}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.6. Orthogonal vectors.

    If the two vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are orthogonal, then \(\theta = \pi/2\text{,}\) which means \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0\text{.}\) Conversely, if \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are two vectors such that \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0\text{,}\) then \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are orthogonal.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0 \iff \mathbf u \perp \mathbf v.\tag{3.3.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.7. A right triangle detected with the dot product.

    Consider the triangle with vertices \(A(1,-1)\text{,}\) \(B(1,1)\text{,}\) and \(C(2,0)\text{.}\) Show that this is a right triangle using the dot product and the orthogonality criterion (3.3.6).
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{CA} \amp = \langle 1-2,\,-1-0\rangle = \langle -1,-1\rangle \\ \overrightarrow{CB} \amp = \langle 1-2,\,1-0\rangle = \langle -1,1\rangle \\ \overrightarrow{CA}\cdot\overrightarrow{CB} \amp = 1 - 1 = 0 \implies \overrightarrow{CA} \perp \overrightarrow{CB}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.9. The remaining angles of the triangle.

    For the triangle of Example 3.3.7 (shown again in Figure 3.3.8), calculate the remaining angles.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For the angle between the sides \(\overrightarrow{AC}\) and \(\overrightarrow{AB}\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{AC} \amp = \langle 2-1,\,0+1\rangle = \langle 1,1\rangle \implies \|\overrightarrow{AC}\| = \sqrt2 \\ \overrightarrow{AB} \amp = \langle 1-1,\,1+1\rangle = \langle 0,2\rangle \implies \|\overrightarrow{AB}\| = 2 \\ \overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} \amp = 0 + 2 = 2 \\ \cos\theta \amp = \frac{\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}}{\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|\|\overrightarrow{AC}\|} = \frac{2}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Properties of the Dot Product.

    Fact 3.3.10.

    If \(\mathbf u\text{,}\) \(\mathbf v\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf w\) are any vectors and \(c\) is a scalar, then
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle (c\mathbf u)\cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf u \cdot (c\mathbf v) = c(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \mathbf u \cdot (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) = \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v + \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf u = \|\mathbf u\|^2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \mathbf 0 \cdot \mathbf u = 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Write \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1,u_2,u_3\rangle\text{,}\) \(\mathbf v = \langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf w = \langle w_1,w_2,w_3\rangle\text{.}\) Each property follows from (3.3.5) together with the corresponding property of real numbers.
    🔗
    1. Multiplication of real numbers is commutative, so
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 = v_1u_1+v_2u_2+v_3u_3 = \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Since \(c\mathbf u = \langle cu_1,cu_2,cu_3\rangle\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} (c\mathbf u)\cdot \mathbf v \amp = cu_1v_1+cu_2v_2+cu_3v_3 \\ \amp = c(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3) = c(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v), \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      and the same computation applied to \(c\mathbf v = \langle cv_1,cv_2,cv_3\rangle\) gives \(\mathbf u \cdot (c\mathbf v) = c(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Adding componentwise and then distributing,
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \mathbf u \cdot (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) \amp = u_1(v_1+w_1) + u_2(v_2+w_2) + u_3(v_3+w_3) \\ \amp = (u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3) + (u_1w_1+u_2w_2+u_3w_3) \\ \amp = \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v + \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Taking \(\mathbf v = \mathbf u\) in (3.3.5), and recalling that \(\|\mathbf u\| = \sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2}\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf u = u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2 = \left(\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2}\right)^2 = \|\mathbf u\|^2. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Every component of \(\mathbf 0\) is zero, so \(\mathbf 0 \cdot \mathbf u = 0u_1+0u_2+0u_3 = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
