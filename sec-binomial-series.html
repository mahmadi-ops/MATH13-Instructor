    Subsection 2.4.1 Binomial Series

    Before we try powers such as \((1+x)^{-2}\) or \(\sqrt{1+x}\text{,}\) let us look at what happens when the exponent is an ordinary positive integer, where nothing beyond multiplication is needed. Multiplying out by hand,
    \begin{align*} (1+x)^1 \amp = 1 + x,\\ (1+x)^2 \amp = 1 + 2x + x^2,\\ (1+x)^3 \amp = 1 + 3x + 3x^2 + x^3,\\ (1+x)^4 \amp = 1 + 4x + 6x^2 + 4x^3 + x^4. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Two features stand out. First, each expansion stops: the power \((1+x)^n\) gives a polynomial with exactly \(n+1\) terms. Second, the coefficients are far from random. Stripping them out and stacking one row per exponent gives the array in Figure 2.4.1.
    🔗
    This array is called Pascal’s triangle. Every row starts and ends with \(1\text{,}\) and each remaining entry is the sum of the two entries just above it — the \(6\) in the bottom row, for instance, is \(3 + 3\text{.}\) That rule alone produces the next expansion with no multiplying at all: the following row is \(1,\, 5,\, 10,\, 10,\, 5,\, 1\text{,}\) and therefore \((1+x)^5 = 1 + 5x + 10x^2 + 10x^3 + 5x^4 + x^5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The triangle tells us what the coefficients are, but not yet why. For that, look at where a term of the expansion actually comes from. Written out in full, the fourth power is
    \begin{equation} (1+x)^4 = (1+x)(1+x)(1+x)(1+x),\tag{2.4.1} \end{equation}
    and multiplying this out means walking through the four factors, picking either the \(1\) or the \(x\) out of each one, and multiplying the four picks together. Every term of the expansion arises that way, from one such set of picks. So to land on \(x^3\) we must take the \(x\) from exactly three of the four factors, and the \(1\) from the single factor left over. The question “what is the coefficient of \(x^3\text{?}\)” has turned into a counting question: in how many ways can we choose \(3\) of the \(4\) factors to hand us an \(x\text{?}\)
    🔗
    There are four such choices and no others, so multiplying out (2.4.1) produces \(x^3\) four times and its coefficient is \(4\) — exactly the entry sitting in the row \(1,\, 4,\, 6,\, 4,\, 1\text{.}\) Nothing here was special to \(x^3\) or to the fourth power. In \((1+x)^n\) the coefficient of \(x^k\) counts the ways of choosing \(k\) of the \(n\) factors to supply an \(x\text{,}\) the remaining \(n-k\) factors supplying a \(1\text{.}\) That count is written \(\binom{n}{k}\) and read “n choose k”, and counting the choices gives the formula
    \begin{equation} \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}.\tag{2.4.2} \end{equation}
    The addition rule we noticed in the triangle is a consequence of the same count.
     1 
    Sort the four rows of Figure 2.4.2 by what the last factor contributes. In three of them it hands over its \(x\text{,}\) so the two remaining \(x\)’s come from the first three factors; there are \(\binom{3}{2} = 3\) ways to pick which two, namely \(\{1,2\}\text{,}\) \(\{1,3\}\) and \(\{2,3\}\text{,}\) and these are precisely the second, third and fourth rows of the figure. In the row left over the last factor hands over its \(1\text{,}\) so all three \(x\)’s come from the first three factors, and there is only \(\binom{3}{3} = 1\) way to do that. Hence \(\binom{4}{3} = \binom{3}{2} + \binom{3}{3} = 3 + 1 = 4\text{,}\) which is why the \(4\) in the row \(1,\, 4,\, 6,\, 4,\, 1\) sits directly below the \(3\) and the \(1\) above it. Nothing depended on the particular numbers: a choice of \(k\) factors out of \(n\) either uses the last factor or it does not, so \(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}\) in general.
    🔗
    Putting the count together with the expansion gives the Binomial Theorem:
    \begin{equation} (1+x)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^k = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!}x^2 + \cdots + nx^{n-1} + x^n.\tag{2.4.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    But what if the exponent is not a positive integer? Could we expand something like \((1+x)^{-2}\text{,}\) or \(\sqrt{1+x} = (1+x)^{1/2}\text{,}\) in the same spirit? At first the idea seems doomed: the factorial formula (2.4.2) makes no sense when \(n = \frac12\text{.}\) However, the equivalent “falling product” form
    \begin{equation} \binom{m}{k} = \frac{m(m-1)(m-2)\cdots(m-k+1)}{k!}\tag{2.4.4} \end{equation}
    asks only that we start at \(m\) and multiply \(k\) numbers, each one less than the previous — and that recipe works for any real number \(m\text{.}\) It also explains why the Binomial Theorem (2.4.3) terminates: when \(m\) is a non-negative integer, the factor \((m-m) = 0\) eventually appears in the product, killing every coefficient from \(k = m+1\) on. When \(m\) is negative or fractional, no factor is ever zero, so the expansion never stops: instead of a polynomial we obtain an infinite power series. This bold step beyond the Binomial Theorem goes back to Newton, and Taylor series give us exactly the right framework to justify it.
    🔗
    Indeed, the Taylor series generated by \(f(x) = (1+x)^m\) at \(x = 0\) is
    \begin{equation} 1 + mx + \frac{m(m-1)}{2!}x^2 + \cdots + \frac{m(m-1)(m-2)\cdots(m-k+1)}{k!}x^k + \cdots,\tag{2.4.5} \end{equation}
    with precisely the coefficients that (2.4.4) predicts. The series (2.4.5) is known as the binomial series.
    🔗

    Binomial Series.

    For \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} (1+x)^m = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{m}{k} x^k\tag{2.4.6} \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \binom{m}{1} = m, \qquad \binom{m}{2} = \frac{m(m-1)}{2}, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{align*} \binom{m}{k} \amp = \frac{m!}{k!(m-k)!}\\ \amp = \frac{m(m-1)(m-2)\cdots(m-k+2)(m-k+1)}{k!} \quad\text{for } k \ge 3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Remark: The binomial series (2.4.6) converges for \(|x| \lt 1\text{.}\) We will not prove this in these notes.
    🔗

    Example 2.4.3. The power is a positive integer.

    Use binomial series to write the function \(f(x) = (x+1)^5\) as a series.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the binomial series (2.4.6) with \(m = 5\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} (1+x)^5 \amp = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{5}{k} x^k\\ \amp = 1 + \binom{5}{1}x + \binom{5}{2}x^2 + \binom{5}{3}x^3 + \binom{5}{4}x^4\\ \amp \quad + \binom{5}{5}x^5 + \binom{5}{6}x^6 + \binom{5}{7}x^7 + \cdots \end{align*}
    🔗
    Note that for \(k = 6\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \binom{5}{6} = \frac{5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0}{6!} = 0, \end{equation*}
    in fact for any \(k \gt 5\text{,}\) it is true that \(\binom{5}{k} = 0\) and therefore
    \begin{align*} (1+x)^5 \amp = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{5}{k} x^k\\ \amp = 1 + \binom{5}{1}x + \binom{5}{2}x^2 + \binom{5}{3}x^3 + \binom{5}{4}x^4 + \binom{5}{5}x^5\\ \amp = 1 + 5x + 10x^2 + 10x^3 + 5x^4 + x^5 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.4. The power is a negative integer.

    Use binomial series to write the function \(f(x) = (x+1)^{-2}\) as a series.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the binomial series (2.4.6) with \(m = -2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1+x)^{-2} = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{-2}{k} x^k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first coefficients are
    \begin{equation*} \binom{-2}{1} = -2, \qquad \binom{-2}{2} = \frac{(-2)(-2-1)}{2} = 3, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and for general \(k\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \binom{-2}{k} \amp = \frac{(-2)(-2-1)(-2-2)(-2-3)\cdots(-2-k+1)}{k!}\\ \amp = (-1)^k \frac{(k+1)!}{k!} = (-1)^k (k+1). \end{align*}
    🔗
    Hence
    \begin{align*} (1+x)^{-2} \amp = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{-2}{k} x^k\\ \amp = 1 - 2x + 3x^2 + \sum_{k=3}^{\infty} (-1)^k (k+1) x^k\\ \amp = 1 - 2x + 3x^2 - 4x^3 + 5x^4 - 6x^5 + \cdots\\ \amp \quad + (-1)^k (k+1) x^k + \cdots \end{align*}
    🔗
    Note that this series converges if \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.5. The power is not an integer.

    Use binomial series to write the function \(f(x) = (x+1)^{\frac12}\) as a series. Compute the five non-zero terms.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the binomial series (2.4.6) with \(m = \frac12\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1+x)^{\frac12} = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{\frac12}{k} x^k \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The coefficients are
    \begin{align*} \binom{\frac12}{1} \amp = \frac12,\\ \binom{\frac12}{2} \amp = \frac{\frac12\left(\frac12 - 1\right)}{2} = \frac{\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)}{2},\\ \binom{\frac12}{3} \amp = \frac{\frac12\left(\frac12 - 1\right)\left(\frac12 - 2\right)}{3!} = \frac{\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac32\right)}{3!},\\ \binom{\frac12}{4} \amp = \frac{\frac12\left(\frac12 - 1\right)\left(\frac12 - 2\right) \left(\frac12 - 3\right)}{4!} = \frac{\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac32\right) \left(-\frac52\right)}{4!}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Hence the five non-zero terms are
    \begin{equation*} (1+x)^{\frac12} = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \binom{\frac12}{k} x^k = 1 + \frac12 x - \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{x^3}{16} - \frac{5x^4}{128} + \cdots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Figure 2.4.6 shows the function \(y = \sqrt{1+x}\) together with the partial sums of this series on the interval of convergence \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\text{.}\)
