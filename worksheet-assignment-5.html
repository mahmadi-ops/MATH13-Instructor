    These problems use the cross product to build normal vectors, areas and orthogonal directions, parametrize plane curves and curves of intersection in space, and intersect lines with planes and measure distances between them. If you would like to review the material first, see The Cross Product, Parametrization of Plane Curves and Lines and Planes in Space.

    1. True or False.

      If \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are two non-zero vectors in \(\R^3\text{,}\) then \(\vec u \cdot (\vec u \times \vec v) = \|\vec u\|^2 + \vec u \cdot \vec v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Justify your answer fully: give a proof if the statement is true, or a counterexample if it is false.
      🔗

    • True.

    • Counterexample: take \(\vec u = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec v = \mathbf i\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i \cdot \underbrace{(\mathbf i \times \mathbf i)}_{\vec 0} = 0, \quad\text{whereas}\quad \|\mathbf i\|^2 + \mathbf i \cdot \mathbf i = 1 + 1 = 2, \end{equation*}
      and \(0 \ne 2\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Counterexample: take \(\vec u = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec v = \mathbf i\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i \cdot \underbrace{(\mathbf i \times \mathbf i)}_{\vec 0} = 0, \quad\text{whereas}\quad \|\mathbf i\|^2 + \mathbf i \cdot \mathbf i = 1 + 1 = 2, \end{equation*}
      and \(0 \ne 2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    2.

    Let \(\mathbf v\) be the vector \(3\,\mathbf i - 4\,\mathbf j + 5\,\mathbf k\text{.}\)
    1. Find a non-zero vector \(\mathbf v'\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find a vector orthogonal to both \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf v'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We need \(\langle a,b,c\rangle\) with \(3a - 4b + 5c = 0\text{.}\) Any solution will do; taking \(c = 0\) leaves \(3a = 4b\text{,}\) so \(a = 4\text{,}\) \(b = 3\) works:
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf v' = 4\,\mathbf i + 3\,\mathbf j, \qquad \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v' = 12 - 12 + 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
      (There are infinitely many correct answers.)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The cross product is orthogonal to both:
      \begin{align*} \mathbf v \times \mathbf v' \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 3 \amp -4 \amp 5 \\ 4 \amp 3 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (0 - 15)\mathbf i - (0 - 20)\mathbf j + (9 + 16)\mathbf k\\ \amp = -15\,\mathbf i + 20\,\mathbf j + 25\,\mathbf k. \end{align*}
      Dividing by \(5\) gives the simpler answer \(-3\,\mathbf i + 4\,\mathbf j + 5\,\mathbf k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose \(\|\vec u\| = 3\text{,}\) \(\|\vec v\| = 4\) and \(\|\vec u\times\vec v\| = 6\text{.}\) Find
    1. \(\left|\vec u\cdot\vec v\right|\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\left\|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec u}\vec v\right\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Let \(\theta\) be the angle between the two vectors, \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{.}\) From \(\|\vec u\times\vec v\| = \|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\sin\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} 6 = (3)(4)\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \frac{6}{12} = \frac12 . \end{equation*}
    Since \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} |\cos\theta| = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac14} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} . \end{equation*}
    (We cannot determine the sign of \(\cos\theta\text{,}\) since \(\theta\) could be \(30^\circ\) or \(150^\circ\text{;}\) this is exactly why the problem asks for the absolute value.) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \left|\vec u\cdot\vec v\right| = \|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\,|\cos\theta| = (3)(4)\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 6\sqrt3 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (b) The projection is \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec u}\vec v = \left(\dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\|\vec u\|^2}\right)\vec u\text{,}\) so its length is
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec u}\vec v\right\| = \frac{\left|\vec u\cdot\vec v\right|}{\|\vec u\|^2}\,\|\vec u\| = \frac{\left|\vec u\cdot\vec v\right|}{\|\vec u\|} = \frac{6\sqrt3}{3} = 2\sqrt3 . \end{equation*}
    As a sanity check, \(2\sqrt3 \approx 3.46\text{,}\) which is less than \(\|\vec v\| = 4\text{,}\) as a shadow must be.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Find the Cartesian equation \(f(x,y) = 0\) for the curve represented by the following parametric equations, then graph it:
    \begin{align*} x(t) \amp = e^{2t},\\ y(t) \amp = e^{t} - 1,\\ 0 \amp \le t \le \ln(2). \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the second equation, \(y + 1 = e^t\text{,}\) and hence
    \begin{equation*} x = e^{2t} = \left(e^{t}\right)^2 = (y+1)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; y + 1 = \sqrt{x} \;\Rightarrow\; y = \sqrt{x} - 1. \end{equation*}
    For the range, note that \(0 \le t \le \ln(2)\) gives
    \begin{equation*} e^0 \le e^t \le e^{\ln(2)} \;\Rightarrow\; 1 \le e^t \le 2 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \le e^t - 1 \le 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \le y \le 1, \end{equation*}
    and correspondingly \(1 \le x \le 4\text{.}\) The curve is the portion of \(y = \sqrt{x} - 1\) from \((1,0)\) to \((4,1)\text{,}\) shown in Figure 5.7.13.

    5.

    Provide a parametrized form of the curve given by
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 4y^2 + 8y - 2\sqrt{21}\,x = 0. \end{equation*}
    Assume that the curve is traced counter-clockwise as the parameter increases.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We complete the squares:
    \begin{align*} \left(x - \sqrt{21}\right)^2 - 21 + 4(y+1)^2 - 4 \amp = 0\\ \left(x - \sqrt{21}\right)^2 + 4(y+1)^2 \amp = 25. \end{align*}
    This is an ellipse centered at \(\left(\sqrt{21}, -1\right)\text{.}\) Setting
    \begin{equation*} x - \sqrt{21} = 5\cos t \quad\text{and}\quad 2(y+1) = 5\sin t \end{equation*}
    gives the counter-clockwise parametrization
    \begin{align*} x(t) \amp = 5\cos t + \sqrt{21}\\ y(t) \amp = \frac52 \sin t - 1\\ 0 \amp \le t \le 2\pi. \end{align*}
    See Figure 5.7.14.

    2. Which axis is perpendicular to the plane \(x = 4\text{?}\)

    7.

    Consider the curve \(25(y+4)^2 + 400(x-1)^2 = 100\text{.}\)
    1. Sketch the curve and label its important points.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Parametrize the entire curve using one parametrization.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Divide both sides by \(100\) to put the equation in standard form:
    \begin{align*} \frac{25(y+4)^2}{100} + \frac{400(x-1)^2}{100} \amp = 1\\ \frac{(y+4)^2}{4} + 4(x-1)^2 \amp = 1\\ \frac{(x-1)^2}{\left(\frac12\right)^2} + \frac{(y+4)^2}{2^2} \amp = 1 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is an ellipse with centre \((1,-4)\text{,}\) semi-axis \(a = \tfrac12\) in the \(x\)-direction and semi-axis \(b = 2\) in the \(y\)-direction. Since \(b \gt a\text{,}\) the major axis is vertical. The four vertices are
    \begin{equation*} \left(\tfrac12, -4\right), \quad \left(\tfrac32, -4\right), \quad (1,-2), \quad (1,-6), \end{equation*}
    and the foci lie on the vertical axis at distance \(c = \sqrt{b^2-a^2} = \sqrt{4-\tfrac14} = \tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{2} \approx 1.94\) from the centre. See Figure 5.7.15.
    🔗
    (b) For a circle \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) we would use \(x = r\cos t\text{,}\) \(y = r\sin t\text{.}\) Here the standard form tells us that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x-1}{\frac12} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{y+4}{2} \end{equation*}
    play the roles of \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\text{,}\) since their squares add to \(1\text{.}\) So we set
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x-1}{\frac12} = \cos t, \qquad \frac{y+4}{2} = \sin t , \end{equation*}
    which gives
    \begin{equation*} x = 1 + \frac12\cos t, \qquad y = -4 + 2\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    Letting \(t\) run over a full period of length \(2\pi\) traces the entire ellipse exactly once, counterclockwise, starting at the right vertex \(\left(\tfrac32,-4\right)\text{.}\)

    8.

    Find the vector equation of the line of intersection of the following planes:
    \begin{align*} 2x + y - z \amp = 6,\\ x - y + 3z \amp = 4. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The normal vectors are \(\vec n_1 = \langle 2, 1, -1\rangle\) and \(\vec n_2 = \langle 1, -1, 3\rangle\text{,}\) so the direction vector of the line of intersection is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf v = \vec n_1 \times \vec n_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \end{vmatrix} = \langle 2, -7, -3\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next we need a point on the line. Setting \(x = 0\) in the equations of the planes gives
    \begin{align*} y - z \amp = 6\\ -y + 3z \amp = 4 \end{align*}
    Adding the two equations gives \(2z = 10\text{,}\) so \(z = 5\) and \(y = 11\text{.}\) Hence the point \((0, 11, 5)\) is on the line, and the vector equation of the line of intersection is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf r(t) = \langle 0, 11, 5\rangle + t\,\langle 2, -7, -3\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    9.

    Find an equation of a plane containing the line \(\mathbf r = \langle -4, 5, -1\rangle + t\langle -4, 6, -3\rangle\) and parallel to the plane \(-3x - y + 2z = 0\text{,}\) in which the coefficient of \(x\) is \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the desired plane is parallel to \(-3x - y + 2z = 0\text{,}\) it has the same normal vector
    \begin{equation*} \vec n = \langle -3, -1, 2\rangle = \langle A, B, C\rangle. \end{equation*}
    (Note that \(\vec n \cdot \langle -4, 6, -3\rangle = 12 - 6 - 6 = 0\text{,}\) so the line is indeed parallel to such a plane.) When \(t = 0\text{,}\) the point \((-4, 5, -1)\) is on the plane, so from the point-normal form,
    \begin{align*} A(x - x_0) + B(y - y_0) + C(z - z_0) \amp = 0\\ \Rightarrow\quad -3(x+4) - (y-5) + 2(z+1) \amp = 0. \end{align*}

    10.

    Calculate the distance between the following two lines:
    \begin{align*} L_1: \amp \quad x = 4 - t, \quad y = 3 + 2t, \quad z = -5 + 3t\\ L_2: \amp \quad x = 5 - t, \quad y = 3 + 2t, \quad z = -5 + 3t \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We notice that the two lines are parallel (both have direction vector \(\mathbf v = \langle -1, 2, 3\rangle\)) and therefore we can find the distance between a point on \(L_2\) and the line \(L_1\text{.}\) Taking \(S(5, 3, -5)\) on \(L_2\) and \(P(4, 3, -5)\) on \(L_1\text{,}\) we get \(\vec{PS} = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle\text{,}\) and \(\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 9} = \sqrt{14}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the cross product,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{PS} \times \mathbf v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ -1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \end{vmatrix} = -3\,\mathbf j + 2\,\mathbf k, \qquad \left\|\vec{PS} \times \mathbf v\right\| = \sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13}, \end{equation*}
    and the distance is
    \begin{equation*} d = \frac{\left\|\vec{PS} \times \mathbf v\right\|}{\|\mathbf v\|} = \frac{\sqrt{13}}{\sqrt{14}}. \end{equation*}

    12.

    Let \(L\) be the line given by
    \begin{equation*} x = 4+t, \qquad y = -1-2t, \qquad z = 5+t . \end{equation*}
    Find the point on \(L\) that is closest to \((-2,2,-1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(Q = (-2,2,-1)\) and let \(P(t) = (4+t,\ -1-2t,\ 5+t)\) be the general point of \(L\text{.}\) The direction vector of the line is \(\vec d = \left\langle 1,\ -2,\ 1\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The closest point is the foot of the perpendicular from \(Q\text{,}\) so we want the value of \(t\) for which \(\vec{QP}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec d\text{.}\) First,
    \begin{align*} \vec{QP} \amp = \left\langle (4+t)-(-2),\ (-1-2t)-2,\ (5+t)-(-1) \right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle 6+t,\ -3-2t,\ 6+t \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now impose \(\vec{QP}\cdot\vec d = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} (6+t)(1) + (-3-2t)(-2) + (6+t)(1) \amp = 0\\ 6 + t + 6 + 4t + 6 + t \amp = 0\\ 18 + 6t \amp = 0\\ t \amp = -3 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Substituting \(t = -3\) into the parametric equations gives the closest point
    \begin{equation*} P(-3) = \left(4-3,\ -1+6,\ 5-3\right) = (1,\ 5,\ 2) . \end{equation*}
    As a check, at \(t=-3\) we get \(\vec{QP} = \left\langle 3,\ 3,\ 3 \right\rangle\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{QP}\cdot\vec d = \left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 1,-2,1\right\rangle = 3 - 6 + 3 = 0 , \end{equation*}
    as required. The distance from \(Q\) to the line is \(\|\vec{QP}\| = \sqrt{9+9+9} = 3\sqrt3\text{.}\)
