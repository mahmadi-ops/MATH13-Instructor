    Subsection 4.6.2 Tangent Plane and Normal Line to a Surface

    In the first example, we find the normal line and the tangent plane to a paraboloid at a given point.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.5. Tangent Plane and Normal Line to a Paraboloid.

    Consider the surface \(z = f(x,y) = -x^2 - y^2\) and the point \(P_0(1, 1, -2)\text{.}\)
    1. Find the equation of the normal line to the surface at \(P_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the equation of the tangent plane to the surface at \(P_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. We begin by computing the normal vector to the surface at \(P_0(1,1,-2)\text{,}\) which is given by \(\mathbf n = \nabla w \Big|_{P_0}\text{,}\) where \(w(x,y,z) = -x^2 - y^2 - z\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \nabla w \amp= \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\imath} + \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\jmath} + \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)\hat{k}\\ \amp= (-2x)\,\hat{\imath} + (-2y)\,\hat{\jmath} + (-1)\,\hat{k}\\ \mathbf n \amp= \nabla w \Big|_{P_0} = -2\,\hat{\imath} - 2\,\hat{\jmath} - \hat{k}. \end{align*}
    Hence, by (4.6.5), the parametric equations of the normal line are
    \begin{align} x(t) \amp= x_0 + f_x(x_0,y_0)\,t\notag\\ \amp= 1 - 2t\notag\\ y(t) \amp= y_0 + f_y(x_0,y_0)\,t\tag{4.6.6}\\ \amp= 1 - 2t\notag\\ z(t) \amp= f(x_0,y_0) - t\notag\\ \amp= -2 - t\notag \end{align}
    for \(t \in \R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    B. Note that we have already calculated the normal vector \(\mathbf n = \langle -2, -2, -1 \rangle\) to the surface at \(P_0(1,1,-2)\text{,}\) and we are ready to write down the equation of the tangent plane:
    \begin{gather} -2x - 2y - z = D,\tag{4.6.7} \end{gather}
    where
    \begin{equation*} D = \mathbf n \cdot \overrightarrow{OP_0} = \langle -2, -2, -1 \rangle \cdot \langle 1, 1, -2 \rangle = -2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore the tangent plane is
    \begin{equation} -2x - 2y - z = -2.\tag{4.6.8} \end{equation}
    Figure 4.6.6 shows the surface, the tangent plane, and the normal line at \(P_0(1,1,-2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Figure 4.6.7 works through this computation from the start: it builds the gradient \(\nabla w\text{,}\) evaluates it at \(P_0\) to get the normal vector, and then draws the normal line and the tangent plane on the surface. Note that the video takes the normal vector in the outward direction, \(\mathbf n = -\nabla w \big|_{P_0} = \langle 2, 2, 1\rangle\text{,}\) which is the opposite of the \(\nabla w \big|_{P_0}\) used above; either choice gives the same normal line and the same tangent plane, since the two vectors differ only by the factor \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The next example puts a tangent plane and its normal vector to work on a rooftop.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.8. A Solar Panel on a Domed Roof.

    The roof of a pavilion is the dome
    \begin{equation*} z = f(x,y) = 5 - \frac{x^2 + y^2}{8}, \end{equation*}
    where \(x\) points east, \(y\) points north, \(z\) is the height, and all three are measured in meters. A flat solar panel is fastened to the roof at \(P_0\left(1, -3, \frac{15}{4}\right)\text{,}\) so that it lies in the tangent plane there (Figure 4.6.9).
    1. Find the equation of the plane of the panel.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In which compass direction does the panel face, and what is its tilt, that is, the angle it makes with the horizontal?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. The partial derivatives of \(f\) are \(f_x = -\frac{x}{4}\) and \(f_y = -\frac{y}{4}\text{,}\) so at \((1,-3)\)
    \begin{align*} f_x(1,-3) \amp= -\frac14,\\ f_y(1,-3) \amp= \frac34. \end{align*}
    By (4.6.3), the plane of the panel is
    \begin{align*} \amp -\frac14(x - 1) + \frac34(y + 3)\\ \amp \qquad {} - \left(z - \frac{15}{4}\right) = 0, \end{align*}
    and multiplying by \(-4\) and collecting the constants gives
    \begin{equation} x - 3y + 4z = 25.\tag{4.6.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    B. By (4.6.4), the vector \(\mathbf n = \left\langle -\frac14, \frac34, -1 \right\rangle\) is normal to the panel. It points down, into the roof, so the panel faces the opposite way, along
    \begin{equation*} -4\,\mathbf n = \langle 1, -3, 4 \rangle . \end{equation*}
    The horizontal part of this vector is \(\langle 1, -3 \rangle\text{:}\) \(3\) meters south for every meter east. So the panel faces a little east of due south, by \(\arctan\frac13 \approx 18^\circ\text{,}\) as Figure 4.6.10 shows.
    🔗
    The tilt of the panel is the angle \(\theta\) between its normal and the vertical direction \(\hat{k}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \cos\theta \amp= \frac{\langle 1,-3,4\rangle \cdot \langle 0,0,1\rangle}{\left|\langle 1,-3,4\rangle\right|}\\ \amp= \frac{4}{\sqrt{26}},\\ \theta \amp\approx 38^\circ . \end{align*}
    Figure 4.6.11 shows why this angle is the tilt: the normal is perpendicular to the panel and \(\hat{k}\) is perpendicular to the horizontal, so the angle between the two arrows is the angle between the panel and the horizontal.
    🔗
    This is nearly the best place on the roof for it. A fixed solar panel in the northern hemisphere collects the most energy over a year when it faces south and its tilt is close to the latitude, and Santa Clara is at latitude \(37^\circ\) north.
