    Subsection 4.8.2 Examples: Functions of Two Variables

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.1 Extrema on an Ellipse

    In the first example, we find the greatest and smallest values of a function whose domain is restricted to a curve in the plane, namely an ellipse.
    🔗

    Example 4.8.6. Extrema of \(f(x,y) = xy\) on an Ellipse.

    Find the greatest and smallest values that the function \(f(x,y) = xy\) takes over the ellipse
    \begin{equation} \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{y^2}{2} = 1.\tag{4.8.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Solution.
    If we picture the graph of \(f(x,y) = xy\text{,}\) a saddle-shaped surface, then restricting the inputs \((x,y)\) to the ellipse traces a curve on that surface, and we are after the highest and lowest points of this curve. We wish to find the extrema of \(f(x,y) = xy\) subject to the constraint (4.8.7), written in the form required by (4.8.6):
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \amp g(x,y) \\ \amp = \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{y^2}{2} - 1 = 0. \end{aligned}\tag{4.8.8} \end{equation}
    🔗
    To apply (4.8.6) we begin by computing the gradients of both functions:
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= y\,\hat{\imath} + x\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \nabla g \amp= \frac{x}{4}\,\hat{\imath} + y\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The Lagrange multiplier equation gives
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= \lambda\,\nabla g\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; y\,\hat{\imath} + x\,\hat{\jmath} = \lambda\frac{x}{4}\,\hat{\imath} + \lambda y\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; y = \lambda\frac{x}{4}, \quad x = \lambda y. \end{align*}
    After substituting \(x = \lambda y\) into \(y = \lambda\frac{x}{4}\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{align*} y = \frac{\lambda^2}{4}\, y \amp\;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = \pm 2\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; x = \pm 2y. \end{align*}
    After plugging \(x = \pm 2y\) into the constraint equation we get
    \begin{align*} \amp g(\pm 2y, y) = \frac{y^2}{2} + \frac{y^2}{2} - 1\\ \amp \quad = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; y^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; y = \pm 1. \end{align*}
    Note that \(y = 0\) is another solution of \(y = \frac{\lambda^2}{4}y\text{;}\) however, when \(y = 0\text{,}\) from \(x = \lambda y\) we know that \(x = 0\text{,}\) and the point \((0,0)\) does not satisfy the constraint equation, i.e. \(g(0,0) = -1 \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore there are four local extrema:
    \begin{align*} (\pm 2, 1)\colon \amp\quad f(2,1) = 2,\\ \amp\quad f(-2,1) = -2,\\ (\pm 2, -1)\colon \amp\quad f(2,-1) = -2,\\ \amp\quad f(-2,-1) = 2. \end{align*}
    Since the ellipse is a closed and bounded curve and \(f\) is continuous, \(f\) attains its absolute extrema over the ellipse, and they are among these four values. The absolute maximum of \(f(x,y) = xy\) over the ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{y^2}{2} = 1\) is \(2\) and its absolute minimum is \(-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Figure 4.8.7 shows the constraint ellipse together with several level curves of \(f\text{:}\) the hyperbolas \(xy = c\text{,}\) with \(|c|\) increasing as the curves move away from the origin. Exactly as promised by (4.8.5), the level curves \(xy = 2\) and \(xy = -2\) are tangent to the ellipse at the four extremal points, and at each of them \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) are collinear. For instance, at \((2,1)\) we have \(\nabla f = \hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}\) and \(\nabla g = \frac{1}{2}\,\hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) so \(\nabla f = 2\,\nabla g\text{,}\) matching the multiplier \(\lambda = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.2 Absolute Extrema on a Circle

    Example 4.8.8. Absolute Extrema on a Circle.

    Find the absolute maximum and minimum of \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 5\text{,}\) given the constraint \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Completing the squares shows that
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \amp f(x,y) = (x-1)^2 \\ \amp \qquad {} + (y+1)^2 + 3, \end{aligned}\tag{4.8.9} \end{equation}
    so the graph of \(f\) is a paraboloid with vertex at \((1,-1,3)\text{.}\) The constraint can be thought of as \(g(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4 = 0\text{,}\) which is a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin in the \(xy\)-plane. Restricting \(f\) to this circle traces a curve on the paraboloid, and the goal is to find the highest and lowest points of that curve. By (4.8.9), the level curves of \(f\) are the circles \((x-1)^2 + (y+1)^2 = c - 3\) centered at \((1,-1)\text{,}\) and we are looking for the smallest and largest values of \(c\) for which the level curve still meets the constraint circle; as Figure 4.8.10 shows, at those values the two circles are tangent and the gradients are collinear, \(\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We compute the gradients:
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= (2x-2)\,\hat{\imath} + (2y+2)\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \nabla g \amp= 2x\,\hat{\imath} + 2y\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
    The Lagrange multiplier equation gives
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= \lambda\,\nabla g\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; (2x-2)\,\hat{\imath} + (2y+2)\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp\qquad = 2\lambda x\,\hat{\imath} + 2\lambda y\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp\;\Rightarrow\; x - 1 = \lambda x,\\ \amp\qquad y + 1 = \lambda y. \end{align*}
    Adding the two equations yields
    \begin{equation} x + y = \lambda(x + y).\tag{4.8.10} \end{equation}
    This equation is satisfied if \(\lambda = 1\) or if \(x + y = 0\text{.}\) The case \(\lambda = 1\) is impossible, since substituting it into \(x - 1 = \lambda x\) gives \(-1 = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(y = -x\text{,}\) which we substitute into the constraint:
    \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + y^2 = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x^2 = 4\\ \amp \quad \Rightarrow\; x = -y = \pm\sqrt{2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Therefore the solutions are \((x,y) = (\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2)\) and \((x,y) = (-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2)\text{.}\) Using (4.8.9),
    \begin{align*} \amp f(\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2)\\ \amp \quad = 2(\sqrt 2 - 1)^2 + 3 = 9 - 4\sqrt 2,\\ \amp f(-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2)\\ \amp \quad = 2(\sqrt 2 + 1)^2 + 3 = 9 + 4\sqrt 2. \end{align*}
    Since the circle is closed and bounded and \(f\) is continuous, these are the absolute extrema: \(f(\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2) = 9 - 4\sqrt 2\) is the absolute minimum and \(f(-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2) = 9 + 4\sqrt 2\) is the absolute maximum.
    🔗
    The answer agrees with the geometry of (4.8.9): on the constraint circle, \(f\) equals \(3\) plus the square of the distance to the point \((1,-1)\text{.}\) The point of the circle farthest from \((1,-1)\) is \((-\sqrt 2, \sqrt 2)\text{,}\) at distance \(2 + \sqrt 2\text{,}\) giving \(f = 3 + (2+\sqrt 2)^2\text{,}\) which is \(9 + 4\sqrt 2\text{,}\) and the nearest point is \((\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2)\text{,}\) at distance \(2 - \sqrt 2\text{,}\) giving \(f = 3 + (2-\sqrt 2)^2\text{,}\) which is \(9 - 4\sqrt 2\text{.}\) Note also that in Figure 4.8.10 the gradients \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) point in the same direction at both extrema, but with different multipliers: from \(x - 1 = \lambda x\text{,}\) we get \(\lambda = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\) at the minimum and \(\lambda = 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\) at the maximum.
    🔗
    The video below animates this picture. The completed-square form (4.8.9) shows that, with no constraint, \(f\) would take its least possible value \(3\) at the vertex \((1,-1)\)—the bottom of the paraboloid. But that vertex lies inside the constraint circle (a distance \(\sqrt 2 \lt 2\) from the origin), so it is not available to us here: the method of Lagrange multipliers instead locates the highest and lowest points of \(f\) along the circle. As the level circles of \(f\) expand outward from \((1,-1)\text{,}\) the first one to reach the constraint circle gives the constrained minimum \(9 - 4\sqrt 2\) and the last gives the maximum \(9 + 4\sqrt 2\text{;}\) at each of these contacts the two circles are tangent and \(\nabla f\) is parallel to \(\nabla g\text{.}\)

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.3 Two Applications

    We close the two-variable examples with two problems from outside the classroom: one from a sawmill, and one from the beach that turns out to be a law of optics.
    🔗

    Example 4.8.11. The Strongest Beam Cut from a Log.

    A sawmill cuts a rectangular beam from a round log \(12\) inches in diameter. The strength of a beam, that is, the load it can carry before it breaks in bending, is proportional to its width times the square of its depth:
    \begin{equation*} S = k\,w\,h^2, \end{equation*}
    where the constant \(k \gt 0\) depends only on the wood. What width \(w\) and depth \(h\) give the strongest beam?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The largest beam has its four corners on the edge of the log, so its diagonal is a diameter (Figure 4.8.12) and \(w^2 + h^2 = 12^2\text{.}\) Since \(k\) is a positive constant, the strongest beam is the one that maximizes \(f(w,h) = wh^2\) subject to
    \begin{equation*} g(w,h) = w^2 + h^2 - 144 = 0, \end{equation*}
    with \(w \gt 0\) and \(h \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We compute the gradients:
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= h^2\,\hat{\imath} + 2wh\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \nabla g \amp= 2w\,\hat{\imath} + 2h\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
    The equation \(\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g\) of (4.8.6) gives
    \begin{align*} h^2 \amp= 2\lambda w,\\ 2wh \amp= 2\lambda h. \end{align*}
    Because \(h \neq 0\text{,}\) the second equation says \(\lambda = w\text{,}\) and then the first says \(h^2 = 2w^2\text{.}\) Substituting into the constraint gives \(3w^2 = 144\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} w \amp= 4\sqrt 3 \approx 6.93 \text{ in},\\ h \amp= \sqrt 2\, w = 4\sqrt 6 \approx 9.80 \text{ in}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the maximum. Along the quarter circle \(w^2 + h^2 = 144\) the function \(f\) is positive, and it falls to \(0\) at both ends, where the beam has no width or no depth. So its greatest value is attained in between, at the only point the method found.
    🔗
    Notice that \(h/w = \sqrt 2\) whatever the diameter of the log: the strongest beam is always about \(1.41\) times as deep as it is wide. It is not the square beam, even though the square beam contains the most wood. For this log the square beam has side \(6\sqrt 2 \approx 8.49\) inches and \(wh^2 \approx 611\text{,}\) against \(wh^2 \approx 665\) for the beam found above, which is about \(9\%\) stronger.

    Example 4.8.13. The Lifeguard’s Path and the Law of Refraction.

    A lifeguard at \(A\text{,}\) standing \(a\) meters from the water’s edge, sees a swimmer in trouble at \(B\text{,}\) which is \(b\) meters out from the shore and \(d\) meters down the beach. She runs at speed \(v_1\) on the sand and swims at the slower speed \(v_2\text{.}\) Show that on the fastest route, the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) that the run and the swim make with the perpendicular to the shoreline satisfy
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(x\) be the distance along the shore that she covers while running and \(y\) the distance along the shore that she covers while swimming, as in Figure 4.8.14. The two must add up to \(d\text{,}\) and each leg of the route is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. So we minimize the travel time
    \begin{align*} T(x,y) \amp= \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}}{v_1}\\ \amp\qquad + \frac{\sqrt{b^2 + y^2}}{v_2} \end{align*}
    subject to the constraint
    \begin{equation*} g(x,y) = x + y - d = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here \(\nabla g = \hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) so the equation \(\nabla T = \lambda\,\nabla g\) of (4.8.6) says that both partial derivatives of \(T\) are equal to \(\lambda\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{x}{v_1\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}} \amp= \lambda,\\ \frac{y}{v_2\sqrt{b^2 + y^2}} \amp= \lambda. \end{align*}
    In the two right triangles of the figure,
    \begin{align*} \sin\theta_1 \amp= \frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}},\\ \sin\theta_2 \amp= \frac{y}{\sqrt{b^2 + y^2}}, \end{align*}
    so the two equations say exactly that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \lambda = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2}. \end{equation*}
    A fastest route does exist, because \(T\) grows without bound as the point \(P\) where she enters the water moves far down the beach in either direction; so it is at a point where this condition holds.
    🔗
    Since \(v_2 \lt v_1\text{,}\) the condition forces \(\theta_2 \lt \theta_1\text{:}\) she should not head straight for the swimmer, but run farther along the sand, where she is fast, and shorten the swim. For the numbers of Figure 4.8.14, solving the condition numerically gives \(x \approx 50\) meters. She enters the water only about \(10\) meters short of the point opposite the swimmer and arrives in \(24.8\) seconds, almost \(4\) seconds sooner than along the straight line from \(A\) to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Light obeys the same rule. Passing from air into water, where it travels more slowly, a ray of light follows the path of least time, and the equation above is Snell’s law of refraction. It is the reason a straw looks bent in a glass of water.
