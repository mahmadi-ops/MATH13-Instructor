    Subsection 4.5.2 The Gradient Vector

    Note that we can write the equation of the line through the point \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) with the direction \(\hat{u} = u_1\hat{\imath} + u_2\hat{\jmath}\) as
    \begin{equation} x(s) = x_0 + su_1, \qquad y(s) = y_0 + su_2.\tag{4.5.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    We can use the above relation between \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(s\) and the chain rule, whose dependency diagram is shown in Figure 4.5.9, to compute the directional derivative as follows:
    \begin{align*} \left(D_{\hat{u}} f\right)_{P_0} = \left(\frac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat{u},P_0} \amp= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{P_0}\frac{dx}{ds}\\ \amp\quad + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{P_0}\frac{dy}{ds}\\ \amp= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{P_0}\cdot u_1\\ \amp\quad + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{P_0}\cdot u_2\\ \amp= \left[\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{P_0}\hat{\imath} + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{P_0}\hat{\jmath}\right] \cdot \left(u_1\hat{\imath} + u_2\hat{\jmath}\right). \end{align*}
    🔗
    The vector in the first set of parentheses is known as the gradient vector.
    🔗

    Definition 4.5.8. Gradient Vector.

    The gradient vector of the function \(f(x,y)\) at \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) is the vector
    \begin{equation} \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0} = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{P_0}\hat{\imath} + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{P_0}\hat{\jmath}.\tag{4.5.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The gradient vector and the directional derivative of \(f(x,y)\) at \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) in the direction \(\hat{u} = u_1\hat{\imath} + u_2\hat{\jmath}\) are related via the following dot product:
    \begin{equation} \left(\frac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat{u},\,P_0} = \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0}\cdot\hat{u}.\tag{4.5.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    A fundamental geometric property of the gradient is that, at every point, it is perpendicular to the level curve of \(f\) passing through that point, as the interactive figure below lets you verify. We state and prove this fact now.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.5.10. The Gradient is Perpendicular to the Level Curves.

    Let \(f(x,y)\) be a differentiable function, and let \(C\) be the level curve \(f(x,y) = c\) for a constant \(c\text{.}\) At every point of \(C\) where \(\nabla f \neq \mathbf 0\text{,}\) the gradient vector \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curve \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose the level curve \(C\) is parametrized by a differentiable vector function
    \begin{equation} \mathbf r(t) = \langle x(t),\, y(t)\rangle,\tag{4.5.6} \end{equation}
    and let \(\mathbf v(t) = \langle x'(t),\, y'(t)\rangle\) denote its velocity vector. Because every point of \(C\) lies on the level curve, its coordinates satisfy
    \begin{equation} f\bigl(x(t),\, y(t)\bigr) = c \qquad \text{for all } t.\tag{4.5.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The left-hand side is constant in \(t\text{,}\) so its derivative is zero. Differentiating both sides with respect to \(t\) and applying the chain rule gives
    \begin{align} \frac{d}{dt}\, f\bigl(x(t), y(t)\bigr) \amp= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\frac{dy}{dt}\notag\\ \amp= 0.\tag{4.5.8} \end{align}
    🔗
    We recognize the middle expression as the dot product of the gradient vector with the velocity vector:
    \begin{align} \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right\rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\, \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle \amp= \nabla f \cdot \mathbf v(t)\notag\\ \amp= 0.\tag{4.5.9} \end{align}
    🔗
    Therefore, at each point of \(C\text{,}\) the gradient \(\nabla f\) is orthogonal to the velocity vector \(\mathbf v(t)\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 4.5.11. Since the velocity vector points in the direction of motion along the curve, it is tangent to \(C\text{;}\) hence \(\nabla f\) being orthogonal to \(\mathbf v(t)\) means precisely that \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curve \(C\text{.}\) This holds at every point where \(\nabla f \neq \mathbf 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 4.5.2.1. Checking that the Gradient Meets Level Curves at Right Angles.

    Theorem 4.5.10 claims that \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curve through a point. This activity asks you to verify that claim by hand at one point, and then to test it at many points using the interactive figure that follows.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(f(x,y) = 1 + x^2 + y^2\text{.}\) Find the level curve of \(f\) through \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\) and give a vector tangent to that curve at \(P_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Level curves of this \(f\) are circles centred at the origin. A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to its radius.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Since \(f(1,1) = 3\text{,}\) the level curve is \(1 + x^2 + y^2 = 3\text{,}\) that is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 2\) of radius \(\sqrt 2\text{.}\) The radius at \(P_0\) points along \(\hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) so a tangent vector is \(\mathbf T = -\hat{\imath} + \hat{\jmath}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Compute \(\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)}\) and then the dot product of that gradient with your tangent vector. What does the result say?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\nabla f = 2x\,\hat{\imath} + 2y\,\hat{\jmath}\) gives \(\left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)} = 2\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{align*} \left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)} \cdot \mathbf T \amp= (2)(-1) + (2)(1) = 0 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    A zero dot product between non-zero vectors means the two are orthogonal, so \(\nabla f\) meets the level curve at a right angle at \(P_0\text{,}\) exactly as Theorem 4.5.10 asserts.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Repeat the argument at an arbitrary point \((a,b) \ne (0,0)\) to show the right angle is not a coincidence of the point \((1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The level curve through \((a,b)\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = a^2 + b^2\text{,}\) whose tangent there is \(\mathbf T = -b\,\hat{\imath} + a\,\hat{\jmath}\text{.}\) Since \(\left(\nabla f\right)_{(a,b)} = 2a\,\hat{\imath} + 2b\,\hat{\jmath}\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \left(\nabla f\right)_{(a,b)} \cdot \mathbf T \amp= 2a(-b) + 2b(a) = 0 , \end{align*}
    so the gradient is perpendicular to the level curve at every point other than the origin, where \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\) and no direction is singled out.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Now drag the red point in Figure 4.5.12 to several locations, including points on the axes and points far from the origin. Confirm that the gradient arrow always meets the level curve at a right angle, and describe how the length of the arrow changes as the point moves away from the origin. Can you place the point where the gradient vanishes?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The right angle persists everywhere. The arrow lengthens as the point moves outward, since \(\|\nabla f\| = 2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) grows with the distance from the origin; correspondingly the level curves are spaced more closely there, because \(f\) is climbing faster. At the origin \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\text{:}\) the level curve degenerates to a single point and there is no direction of steepest ascent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    With (4.5.5) in hand, the limit that defined the directional derivative can be set aside: the gradient and a dot product do the same work in a couple of lines. To see the difference, we rework Example 4.5.6 — the same function, point, and direction — and then read the answer geometrically off the surface.
    🔗

    Example 4.5.13. Example II.

    Consider the function \(f(x,y) = 1 + x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) the point \(P_0(1,1)\text{,}\) and the unit vector \(\hat{u} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}\text{.}\) Evaluate the directional derivative \(\left(D_{\hat{u}} f\right)_{P_0}\) and interpret your result.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use \(\left(\dfrac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat{u},P_0} = \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0}\cdot\hat{u}\text{.}\) First we compute the gradient vector (4.5.4):
    \begin{align*} \nabla f \amp= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\hat{\imath} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp= (2x)\,\hat{\imath} + (2y)\,\hat{\jmath},\\ \left(\nabla f\right)_{(1,1)} \amp= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{(1,1)}\hat{\imath} + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{(1,1)}\hat{\jmath}\\ \amp= 2\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Then we take the dot product with \(\hat{u}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align} \left(\frac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat{u},P_0} \amp= \left(\nabla f\right)_{P_0}\cdot\hat{u}\notag\\ \amp= \left(2\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}\right)\cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,\hat{\imath} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,\hat{\jmath}\right)\notag\\ \amp= \frac{4}{\sqrt 2}\notag\\ \amp= 2\sqrt 2,\tag{4.5.10} \end{align}
    which agrees with the limit computation in Example 4.5.6. See Figure 4.5.14 for a geometrical interpretation of the directional derivative. Note that the unit vector \(\hat{u} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\imath} + \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\hat{\jmath}\) is the direction vector of the line \(y = x\) located in the \(xy\)-plane. The plane \(x - y = 0\) contains this line and is orthogonal to the \(xy\)-plane. The intersection of the surface \(z = 1 + x^2 + y^2\) and the plane \(x - y = 0\) is the parabola shown in the figure. Finally, the directional derivative of \(f(x,y) = 1 + x^2 + y^2\) at the point \((1,1)\) corresponds to the slope of the tangent line to the surface \(z = 1 + x^2 + y^2\) at \((1,1,3)\) that is located in the plane \(x - y = 0\text{.}\)
